*I want to know what a data center takes from the place it's built. A yearly gallon count needs a water balance and local supply conditions before it can answer that. About a 15-minute read.*

## The reported number

### 1. Thirteen million gallons in household and city units

A data center's annual water total can't tell me whether its neighbors will run short. I need to know what that number measures and what the local system can supply.

The story that prompted this was a [September 2026 KOLN report](https://www.1011now.com/2026/09/30/more-questions-than-answers-about-lincolns-google-data-center-water-electricity-usage/) about Google's Lincoln, Nebraska facility. KOLN reported 13.299 million gallons for 2025, extracted from an improperly redacted filing. The [replacement state filing](https://ecmp.nebraska.gov/PublicAccess/index.html?MyQueryID=340) confirms the reporting year but redacts the values. I'm treating 13.299 million as the amount the news report gave, rather than an independently verified meter reading.

KOLN already compares that amount with one day of Lincoln's water use. That's a useful start. A household comparison gives me another way to understand the volume.

Take a family with two adults and two children. [EPA's published household benchmark](https://www.epa.gov/watersense/statistics-and-facts) is 82 gallons per person per day, including outdoor use. That benchmark comes from 2015 observations; it isn't a new survey of families in 2026. Applying the same allowance to four people gives 328 gallons a day, or about 120,000 gallons over a 365-day year.

On those assumptions, the amount KOLN reported equals about 111 years of water supplied to that illustrative household. Or one year for 111 such households.

[Lincoln's 2025 drinking-water report](https://www.lincoln.ne.gov/files/sharedassets/public/v/12/ltu/utilities/water-system/water-quality-report.pdf) gives an average municipal supply of about 37.6 million gallons a day. The reported data-center amount is equivalent to roughly eight and a half hours of that supply. Against the [broader New York City supply system](https://www.nyc.gov/site/dep/water/drinking-water.page), which delivers more than a billion gallons daily and includes upstate customers, it's under twenty minutes.

These comparisons describe the same volume. None tells me how much water evaporated, how much returned locally, or whether the pipes and source can meet demand on a dry summer afternoon.

There's also an operating-stage problem: [Google's own timeline](https://datacenters.google/locations/nebraska/) says Lincoln came online in 2026. Its reported 2025 use can't establish a mature annual operating rate. And the reported site total doesn't isolate AI workloads. A Google data center's water total doesn't become an AI-only footprint because an AI debate links to it.

### 2. Where the water goes

A household equivalent is a useful volume conversion. Assessing local water loss requires a different calculation.

*Withdrawal* is water taken from a source. *Consumption* is the portion that doesn't return to the immediate water environment over the accounting period. Evaporation is one way cooling consumes water. A return to another watershed, or after the period when the source needs it, needs its own accounting.

For a deliberately simple example, imagine two facilities that each withdraw 100 units. One returns 20 units to the same source environment and evaporates 80. The other returns 80 and evaporates 20. Their withdrawals match; their local consumption differs by a factor of four.

Those fractions are invented to explain the distinction. They aren't estimates for Google or for household plumbing. Comparing household water supplied with a facility's evaporative consumption would compare two different quantities, even if both were measured in gallons.

Liquid cooling inside a server doesn't specify how the facility releases heat outdoors. As [DOE's cooling-water guide](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/femp/cooling-water-efficiency-opportunities-federal-data-centers) explains, an internal liquid loop transfers heat to another system. That final heat-rejection system determines whether cooling water evaporates at the site.

In a [September 2026 preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.25437v1), Garrett Alston, Nancy Love, and Rabab Haider model facility cooling, electricity generation, and local water conditions in Michigan. Their scenarios can shift water consumption between facilities and generators. The results depend on assumed loads and historical inputs.

That gives a water claim at least two operational boundaries: the facility and the electricity supplying it. Construction and equipment manufacturing would expand the boundary again. A site cooling-water number covers only part of that assessment.

Even a complete water balance for Lincoln's reported year wouldn't answer what a much larger, fully operating campus might need. That requires a measure of computing load.

## A larger campus

### 3. From 100 megawatts to eight gigawatts

Power measures how fast electricity is being used. Energy measures how much is used over time. A one-gigawatt rating by itself supplies neither an annual electricity bill nor an annual water bill.

One gigawatt is 1,000 megawatts. A [watt-hour](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/electricity/measuring-electricity.php) is one watt used for one hour. Keeping a one-gigawatt electrical load running for all 8,760 hours of a 365-day year uses 8.76 terawatt-hours (TWh). A TWh is a billion kilowatt-hours.

For the scenarios below, capacity means *IT power*: the servers, storage, and networking equipment. IT capacity excludes cooling and other facility overhead. The average electrical-load fraction is the year's average IT power divided by that capacity; it isn't a GPU utilization percentage.

| Hypothetical IT capacity | IT electricity at continuous full load | Annual US residential-account equivalents |
|---|---:|---:|
| 100 MW | 0.876 TWh | About 85,000 |
| 1 GW | 8.76 TWh | About 850,000 |
| 5 GW | 43.8 TWh | About 4.2 million |
| 8 GW | 70.08 TWh | About 6.8 million |
| 10 GW | 87.6 TWh | About 8.5 million |

The household electricity denominator comes from [EIA's 2024 residential data](https://www.eia.gov/electricity/sales_revenue_price/pdf/table_5A.pdf): 863 kWh per customer account per month, approximately 10,356 kWh over a year. It's an account average, not a measurement of our particular family of four. These equivalents cover IT electricity alone.

At a 50% average electrical load, halve the table's energy figures. To get total facility energy, multiply IT energy by *power usage effectiveness*, or PUE. [Google's explanation](https://datacenters.google/efficiency/) defines that overhead relationship. A hypothetical PUE of 1.2 means 20% more electricity than the IT equipment alone.

Gigawatt-scale proposals are real, but their announced capacities need their original labels:

| Project | Operator's announced capacity | Stage and boundary |
|---|---|---|
| [Meta Prometheus](https://engineering.fb.com/2026/02/09/data-center-engineering/building-prometheus-how-backend-aggregation-enables-gigawatt-scale-ai-clusters/) | 1 GW | Capacity once complete; the announcement doesn't specify the IT/facility split |
| [Meta Hyperion expansion](https://datacenters.atmeta.com/2026/07/deepening-our-investment-in-richland-parish-louisiana/) | 5 GW of compute capacity | Expansion target; the announcement doesn't reconcile the IT/facility boundary |
| [OpenAI PORTS-Pike](https://openai.com/index/openai-joins-ports-pike-project/) | Approximately 8 GW IT | Contracted future capacity; first 800 MW expected in 2028 |

The generic table isn't a forecast for those projects. It shows what the stated electrical load would require if it ran continuously. An announcement of future capacity needs operating measurements before it becomes an annual-use claim.

Annual IT electricity equals IT capacity multiplied by the year's average electrical-load fraction and the number of hours in the year. Turning it into water still needs a cooling-water intensity.

### 4. A gigawatt doesn't specify a water bill

Water usage effectiveness, or WUE, expresses site water use per unit of IT electricity. The name alone doesn't settle whether the numerator is withdrawal or consumption. The dataset's definition does.

[AWS defines its reported WUE](https://sustainability.aboutamazon.com/products-services/aws-cloud) as liters withdrawn per kWh of IT load. Its 2025 regional averages include 0.02 in Stockholm, 1.22 in Tokyo, 1.57 in Singapore, and 2.85 in Jakarta. That's roughly a 140-fold difference between the lowest and highest of those regions, using the same provider, year, and metric.

These are operator-reported averages across AWS workloads. They aren't measurements of the same AI prompt in four places, and they don't rank ecological harm. They demonstrate how much the water intensity can vary even before local scarcity enters the calculation.

For a hypothetical one-gigawatt IT load operating continuously, apply AWS's reported 2025 global average of 0.12 liters per IT kWh. The arithmetic is 8.76 billion kWh times 0.12 liters, which gives about 1.05 billion liters, or 277.7 million US gallons of annual site withdrawals.

That's about 2,300 illustrative four-person household-years of water supplied. It's a substantial industrial demand. It also changes directly with the electrical load and the chosen withdrawal intensity.

The calculator lets those assumptions vary separately so their effects are visible. A real cooling design can tie water intensity and electricity overhead together; arbitrary slider combinations aren't validated engineering designs. The water output also excludes power-generation water and hardware manufacturing.

I wouldn't assign this AWS-based scenario to Prometheus, Hyperion, or PORTS-Pike. Their actual water demand needs their own design and operating data, with compatible power boundaries. Multiplying an unspecified total-facility GW rating by a per-IT-kWh factor would mix denominators.

The one-gigawatt reference scenario gives us an annual withdrawal amount. A local utility still has to deliver water when the facility needs it.

### 5. The summer demand a yearly total hides

The reported Lincoln amount looks small beside the city's yearly supply. That comparison doesn't reveal capacity remaining after existing customers, operating limits, and reserves. Annual water already delivered isn't a measurement of headroom.

For another explicit toy model, assume the one-gigawatt reference facility gets 100% of its water from a utility. Its annual withdrawals are 277.7 million gallons. Suppose that utility has one million gallons a day of delivery capacity available to this new customer after other commitments and reserves.

Spread the facility's water evenly through the year and its demand is about 0.76 million gallons a day. Double the rate for three summer months and reduce the other nine months to preserve the annual total. Summer demand reaches about 1.52 million gallons a day, above the assumed delivery headroom.

{/* FIGURE-5 */}

The model treats months as equal-duration blocks for simple arithmetic. It doesn't claim that this is any actual data center's seasonal profile. The utility supply assumption matters too: if it supplied only half the facility's water, only that half would belong against its delivery-capacity limit.

The horizontal line measures utility delivery capacity. It isn't a limit on sustainable watershed depletion. A facility can fit within the pipes' capacity while its source or downstream users face a separate water problem; evaluating that requires source availability, local consumption, and the location and timing of returns.

The integrated [Alston, Love, and Haider model](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.25437v1) tracks monthly water availability and existing consumption as well as the added demand. That approach explains why an annual volume alone can't establish local water risk. It doesn't establish Lincoln's actual risk or spare capacity.

I want that distinction carried into any city comparison. Eight hours of average supply tells me the scale of a reported volume. A dry-month water balance and an actual utility capacity study would tell me whether a new campus can be served without displacing another use or needing upgrades.

After those water checks, the reference campus still has an electricity demand. A cooling design that reduces site water doesn't settle what happens at the generators.

### 6. Electricity supply and carbon accounting

[OpenAI's PORTS-Pike announcement](https://openai.com/index/openai-joins-ports-pike-project/) describes planned closed-loop, air-cooled systems and says quantitative expected water use will be reported after the design is finalized. The same announcement says later development requires new power plants, including natural-gas generation. The low-water cooling plan and the generation plan both belong in an assessment.

For our generic one-gigawatt IT scenario, continuous operation uses 8.76 TWh a year. At the hypothetical PUE of 1.2, the whole facility uses 10.512 TWh. That is about one million US residential accounts' annual electricity, using the EIA benchmark.

To estimate electricity-related emissions in an inventory, multiply that facility electricity by an appropriate emissions factor. A chosen factor of 400 grams of CO2-equivalent per kWh produces about 4.20 million metric tonnes a year. Choose 50 grams instead and the result is about 0.53 million tonnes.

{/* FIGURE-6 */}

Those factors are illustrative inputs, not measured grid factors for Lincoln or any announced campus. The calculator shows electricity-related inventory emissions. It doesn't calculate chip manufacturing, construction, or the net emissions caused by adding demand to a power system.

[EPA's eGRID guidance](https://www.epa.gov/egrid/frequent-questions-about-egrid) explains why generation mix affects average emissions, and why inventory averages and marginal emissions answer different questions. An average describes the existing mix. Estimating the consequence of another large load requires knowing which generation changes and what infrastructure gets built.

Carbon reports distinguish location-based emissions from market-based emissions. In the [GHG Protocol's accounting definitions](https://ghgprotocol.org/sites/default/files/2025-10/GHG-Protocol-Scope2-Public-Consultation.pdf), location-based figures use geographically averaged generation factors. Market-based figures attribute electricity through contractual instruments. The cited 2025 consultation document reproduces the existing definitions alongside proposed revisions; I'm using those definitions here.

A renewable-electricity contract can affect a company's market-based account. It doesn't, by itself, give me an hourly picture of the generators supplying its site or the emissions caused by its expansion. Those claims need evidence suited to those questions.

The utility-capacity test compares summer water demand with delivery headroom; the emissions calculation multiplies annual facility electricity by the chosen emissions factor. Its stated assumptions produce 277.7 million gallons of annual site withdrawals and 10.512 TWh of facility electricity. With all water supplied by the toy utility, the chosen summer peak exceeds its chosen one-million-gallon daily delivery headroom; the chosen carbon factor yields 4.20 million tonnes.

The delivery calculation identifies a constraint under those assumptions. Local consumptive loss remains unknown because we haven't supplied return flows or source conditions. The carbon calculation remains an inventory scenario because we haven't modeled the power system's response. The calculated withdrawals and electricity use still need assessments of local water loss and the power system's response.

One campus also can't tell us how much electricity the entire industry uses. That needs an industry estimate with its own population and time period.

## Industry totals and individual prompts

### 7. From sector electricity to a prompt

The [IEA's April 2026 assessment](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary) estimates that all data centers used 485 TWh in 2025. Its central projection reaches 950 TWh in 2030, around 3% of projected global electricity demand that year. Those totals include workloads beyond AI, and the 2030 figure is a forecast.

The IEA expresses annual data-center electricity use in terawatt-hours. A terawatt is power; a terawatt-hour is energy. A hypothetical one-terawatt continuous load would require 8,760 TWh a year, roughly eighteen times that 2025 industry estimate. None of the project sources above describes an operating terawatt campus. Swapping TW and TWh would make a very different claim.

An everyday AI user's question is smaller: how much electricity did this particular task require? The IEA also describes substantial differences between simple text generation and reasoning, video, or agentic workloads. A count of requests doesn't specify the amount of computing work.

Changing the mix of tasks can change average electricity per task, even if each task's energy requirement stays the same. For a toy calculation, define a task as one repetition of the same fixed workload (with the same output specification), and keep the workload mix unchanged. Required IT electricity equals task count times mean electricity per task.

If electricity per task halves while the number of these tasks triples, required electricity grows by 50%. To hold electricity flat with three times the work, each task must use at most one-third of the previous energy. The interactive curve shows that threshold across different growth assumptions.

{/* FIGURE-7 */}

The baseline is our continuous one-gigawatt IT scenario's 8.76 TWh. A 1.5-times result means 13.14 TWh required, which needs more capacity or more elapsed time than that original full-load campus provides in a year. It doesn't mean unchanged equipment somehow exceeds its capacity.

This is electricity arithmetic for a fixed workload, rather than an explanation of the IEA forecast or a claim that efficiency causes higher demand. Water would need its own per-task estimate or a separately stated water-intensity assumption.

So a useful aggregate estimate needs a defined workload mix and mean energy per task. A useful prompt estimate needs the relevant task, model, output, location, and accounting boundary. A campus capacity announcement and an annual regional WUE can't supply those details on their own.

### 8. The measurements I want published

For an everyday AI user, I wouldn't turn a campus water headline into a precise environmental price for each question. The necessary allocation data often isn't in the headline. I also wouldn't use an efficient example task as a guarantee that a long agent run or generated video has the same footprint.

My own test would start with the task's usefulness. Is the output doing work I value? Then I'd ask what evidence exists for that workload's resource demand. That's a judgment about a particular use; it doesn't cancel a community's separate need to assess a large facility.

For that facility assessment, I want operators to publish:

- Monthly withdrawals, local consumption, return destinations, and water sources, with drinking water and reused water identified.
- Annual IT and total-facility electricity, average electrical load, and which stage of construction or operation the numbers describe.
- Projected demand at full buildout, including peak water-delivery requirements and the utility's capacity after existing commitments and reserves.
- Electricity emissions with the accounting method named, alongside evidence for claims about hourly supply or the consequences of new demand.
- For per-task claims, the defined workload, output specification, averaging method, and included resource boundaries.

Those disclosures would let readers test the comparisons instead of guessing what a rounded annual total includes. Withdrawal intensity measures the site water required per unit of IT electricity. Local return flows, utility headroom, and generation changes determine what else that demand requires from the surrounding systems.

KOLN's reported 2025 water total for Google's Lincoln site is 13.299 million gallons. The household and city equivalents still help me understand the volume. They still don't establish an AI-only total, the facility's mature operating demand, or its effect on local water availability.

I want the monthly water balance and the full-buildout demand public before a community is asked to accommodate a new campus. Residents need numbers they can compare with the water and electricity their own systems can actually provide.
