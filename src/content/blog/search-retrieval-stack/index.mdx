*A walk through the history of search indexes, ending at two production deployments from the same company in the same year that reached opposite conclusions about replacing the same component. About a 55-minute read.*

## Act 1 — The only question

### 1. One query, and the scan you cannot afford

Type `waterproof hiking boots wide toe box under $150` into a search box and something answers in a couple of hundred milliseconds. Behind the box is a pile of documents. Call it a hundred million, which is small for a web index and large for a product catalog.

The obvious way to answer is to score every document and sort. Nobody does that, and the reason isn't that it's slow in some hand-wavy sense. It's slow by an amount somebody wrote down.

Ding and Suel measured it in 2011 on GOV2, a crawl of 25.2 million government web pages. Scoring every document containing any query term cost 225.7 milliseconds per query and fully scored 3,815,676 documents. Their [block-max index](https://research.engineering.nyu.edu/~suel/papers/bmw.pdf) answered the same queries in 27.9 milliseconds, fully scoring 21,921 of them.

Look at those two ratios, because they are not the same ratio. The work fell by a factor of about 174. The time fell by a factor of about 8. Skipping documents is enormously effective and nowhere near free, and the distance between those two numbers is where most of the last thirty years of engineering went.

{/* TODO: Fig 1: ScanCost. Documents fully scored, and separately latency, for exhaustive OR vs WAND vs block-max on GOV2, dated. Two axes, not one. */}

So every index ever built answers one question: how do you avoid comparing the query to everything? Trees answer it by cutting space into regions. Inverted indexes answer it by only visiting documents that contain your words. Graphs answer it by walking toward the answer instead of scanning for it. Different answers, same question, and each one buys its speed by giving something up.

Here is the part that took me a while to see.

The thing each generation gives up is almost never the thing its benchmark measures.

An inverted index is not only a fast way to match text. It also sits inside an operational pipeline where someone can inject a term at two in the afternoon and have it take effect by three, without retraining anything. That capability does not appear in nDCG. It does not appear in recall@100. It does not appear in any leaderboard. And in at least one documented production system, it is the reason the inverted index outlived a model that beat it on the metrics anyone had bothered to run.

That is what this post is about. We will walk the history from posting lists to generative retrieval, and at every step ask the same four questions.

What was the incumbent quietly doing that no benchmark measured? What did the challenger win on? Did the challenger preserve that property, or route around it? And what actually happened when someone tried to ship it?

Two notes on how to read what follows, both of which matter.

The boot query is a worked example. It carries the mechanics: it will show you what each generation of machinery can and cannot do, and I will run it through every act. It is not evidence for anything. When I claim something about production systems, the evidence is a cited deployment, not the boots.

And the metrics arrive as they become necessary rather than in a primer up front. When a number appears, I will say what it measures and, where it matters, what it fails to measure. That second half turns out to be the more interesting one: by the end of this post there is a documented case where an automated evaluation ranked systems in almost exactly the reverse of how humans ranked them, and it did so precisely among the systems anyone would actually be choosing between.

The answers are more interesting than "vectors won."

## Act 2 — The lexical machine

### 2. The posting list, and why rare words are the whole trick

The structure that answers the boot query has not changed much since the 1970s. Ding and Suel describe it in one sentence: "An inverted index consists of many inverted lists, where each inverted list Lw is a list of postings describing all places where term w occurs in the collection."

That's it. For every word in the vocabulary, keep a list of the documents containing it. To answer `waterproof hiking boots wide toe box under $150`, you don't look at a hundred million documents. You look at the list for `waterproof`, the list for `hiking`, the list for `toe`, and you work with what's on them.

Why this is fast has nothing to do with clever data structures and everything to do with English. Most words are rare. The list for `waterproof` is a tiny slice of the corpus, and the list for a brand name is smaller still. You skipped the scan because your query happened to name a few uncommon things.

{/* TODO: Fig 2: PostingList. Term to posting list, docID plus frequency, decompressible blocks of 64 or 128. */}

Which tells you exactly where it stops being fast. Some words are not rare at all, and the same paper says so plainly: "The inverted lists of common query terms may consist of many millions or even billions of postings." Add `the` to your query and you have undone the trick. This is why every production system carries a stopword policy, and why the interesting engineering is always about the frequent terms rather than the rare ones.

Two implementation details matter later, so they are worth noticing now.

The lists are compressed, and the compression is not an optimization someone added at the end. On GOV2, 426 GB of raw pages became an 8,759 MB index. Twenty-fold, before anyone talks about vectors.

And the compressed lists are cut into blocks, "of, say, 64 or 128 docIDs, such that each block can be decompressed separately." Nothing in this section needs that fact. Section 4 is built on it.

### 3. BM25's two dials

Having found the documents, you have to order them, and the function that does it is thirty years old and still the thing you have to beat.

BM25 has more moving parts than it needs, but only two of them are ideas. The rest is bookkeeping.

The first idea is that a term should stop paying out. If a page says `waterproof` once, that tells you something. If it says `waterproof` forty times, that tells you almost nothing more, and quite possibly tells you the page is trying too hard. Robertson and Zaragoza name the behaviour directly: "any one term's contribution to the document score cannot exceed a saturation point (the asymptotic limit), however, frequently it occurs in the document."

The dial that controls how fast the payout flattens is `k1`, and the two extremes are worth holding in your head, because the middle follows from them. "for high k, increments in tf continue to contribute significantly to the score, whereas for low k, the additional contribution of a newly observed occurrence tails off very rapidly." High `k1`, repetition keeps helping. Low `k1`, the second mention is nearly worthless.

{/* TODO: Fig 3: Bm25Dials. Small multiples: three k1 saturation curves, three b length-normalization states. */}

The second idea is that longer documents need handicapping, but not fully, and the reason it's not fully is the interesting bit. A document can be long because it's padded, in which case its term counts are inflated and should be divided down. Or it can be long because it genuinely covers more ground, in which case dividing down is exactly wrong. The paper calls these the verbosity and scope hypotheses and refuses to pick: "The verbosity hypothesis suggests that we should simply normalise any observed tf s by dividing by document length. The scope hypothesis, on the other hand, at least in its extreme version, suggests the opposite."

So you get a dial instead of a decision. "setting b = 1 will perform full document-length normalisation, while b = 0 will switch normalisation off."

Now the part people find unsatisfying. The model does not tell you what to set either dial to. "Concerning the internal parameters, the model provides no guidance on how these should be set. This may be regarded as a limitation of the model." Thirty years of empirical work has landed on roughly `0.5 < b < 0.8` and `1.2 < k1 < 2`, with the honest caveat that the optimum depends on your documents and your queries.

Here's the thing I expected to matter and doesn't.

You may have heard that BM25 is not one algorithm, that implementations differ, and that this is why your numbers never match the paper's. The first half is true. Kamphuis and colleagues took eight variants and tested them properly, and the answer to their own title question is in their conclusion: "no, it does not". Their measurement: "Both an ANOVA and Tukey's HSD show no significant differences between any variant, on all test collections." Differences from something as mundane as your stopword list are larger.

What does differ is more boring and more actionable. Lucene compresses document length into a single byte so it can precompute the normalization term for all 256 possible values. And Anserini, the research toolkit built on Lucene, ships `k1 = 0.9, b = 0.4` where Lucene itself ships `1.2` and `0.75`. Same algorithm, same library underneath, different numbers out. If your BM25 doesn't match a published BM25, look there before you go looking at the formula.

### 4. Pruning fixed the cost, and could not fix the meaning

Section 2 left you with a problem. The posting list for a common term runs to millions of entries, and scoring all of them is the thing we're trying to avoid.

The fix is older than most of the machine learning in this post, and it is the same fix that the last act of this post is about.

You keep a running threshold: the score of the tenth-best document you've seen so far. You also keep, for each posting list, the largest score any single document could possibly get from that term. Add up those maxima for the lists you're considering, and if the total can't reach your threshold, you don't need to look. You skip. [Broder and colleagues](https://research.ibm.com/publications/efficient-query-evaluation-using-a-two-level-retrieval-process) built this in 2003 and reported that it "significantly reduces the total number of full evaluations by more than 90%, almost without any loss in precision or recall."

That is a cheap approximate pass in front of an expensive exact one. Hold onto that shape. It is the entire architecture of act 6, arriving twenty years early and with no neural networks in it.

Block-max sharpened it. Ding and Suel noticed why the original leaves value on the table: "skipping in WAND is limited because it uses the maximum impact scores over the entire lists, which can be much larger than average." One outlier document at the far end of a list poisons the bound for the whole list. So they stored a maximum per block instead of per list, using the block structure from section 2, and the bound got tight enough to skip most of the postings rather than some of them.

{/* TODO: Fig 4: BlockMaxSkip. List-level and block-level maxima against a running threshold; which blocks get skipped. */}

The numbers from section 1 are these two techniques. Exhaustive scoring fully evaluated 3,815,676 documents per query. WAND cut that to 178,391. Block-max cut it to 21,921, which is 0.57% of what the exhaustive pass touched.

So by 2011, before dense retrieval existed as a practical thing, lexical search could rank a 25-million-document corpus in under 30 milliseconds and could tell you exactly which documents it had skipped and why.

And it still could not find a boot described as having a roomy forefoot when the query said `wide toe box`.

That is the point of this section. Dynamic pruning is one of the genuinely good ideas in information retrieval, and it made term matching affordable at a scale nobody had managed before. It did not move the semantic limit by a single inch, because it was never about meaning. Every document WAND skips is a document that didn't contain your words. If the right answer never contained your words, no amount of pruning will find it.

### 5. The two things term matching cannot do

Our query has three parts, and the machine we have built handles exactly one of them well.

`waterproof` and `hiking` it handles. Those are words, they're reasonably rare, and documents about waterproof hiking boots contain them.

`wide toe box` is where it fails, and the failure has a name and a measurement. In 1987, Furnas, Landauer, Gomez and Dumais ran a study on how people spontaneously name things, across five different application domains, and found: "In every case two people favored the same term with probability <0.20." Their per-domain numbers run from .07 to .18. Two people describing the same thing pick the same word less than one time in five.

Read that number carefully, because it is easy to over-claim. It is about spontaneous naming across five domains in 1987, not about modern search queries. But the shape of the problem is exactly the one our query has. A boot listing that says `roomy forefoot`, or `generous last`, or `available in wide`, is describing the thing the user asked for, in words the user did not use. An inverted index will never retrieve it, and no amount of tuning `k1` will change that, because the document is not on the posting list.

You can attack it from the document side. Add fields, and weight them, which is what BM25F does. The mechanism is worth one paragraph because people get it wrong: you do not score each field separately and add up the scores. You pool the weighted term frequency across fields first, apply saturation once to the total, and only then combine across terms. Scoring fields separately assumes that being about boots in the title is a different property from being about boots in the body, which the authors call "a little unreasonable."

That helps. Titles and product attributes are exactly where `wide` lives in a well-structured catalog.

It does not help with `under $150`.

That clause is not a term. There is no posting list for it. `$150` appears in the query, `$149.99` appears in the document, and they share no token. You can hack around it by bucketing prices into tokens, and people did, and it is miserable. The honest description is that this is a different kind of question wearing the same clothes: a constraint over a numeric attribute, not a request for text similarity, and the fact that it arrived in the same input box is an accident of user interface.

So we have two failures, and they are not the same failure. One is about meaning: the right document exists and uses different words. The other is about structure: the right document is identifiable exactly, and the mechanism for finding it isn't a text match at all.

Almost everything in the next four acts is a response to the first one. The second turns out to be harder to keep hold of, and it is the one that breaks things later.

One more thing before we leave. Let's say the first result set disappoints, and our user does what users actually do: gets specific.

`Salomon Quest 4 GTX wide 10.5`

Term matching is superb at this. Every token is rare, the brand and model are nearly unique, and the posting lists are short. This query is the best case for an inverted index and it will stay the best case for the rest of the post. Remember it. In act 3 we will build something that handles the first query beautifully and this one noticeably worse.

## Act 3 — The vector turn

### 6. One vector per document, and the objective that puts meaning in it

The response to the vocabulary problem is to stop matching words and start matching positions in a space.

The architecture is simple enough to describe in a sentence. [Karpukhin and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906) used "two independent BERT (Devlin et al., 2019) networks (base, uncased)" and took the `[CLS]` output, giving 768 numbers per query and 768 per passage. Score is the dot product. Two encoders, no interaction between them, which is the property that makes the whole thing possible: you can encode every document offline and store the results, because encoding a document doesn't depend on the query.

What people skip is why nearby vectors mean anything at all. Nothing about a neural network makes similar text land in similar places. That has to be trained in, and the training objective states the goal outright: "The goal is to create a vector space such that relevant pairs of questions and passages will have smaller distance (i.e., higher similarity) than the irrelevant ones, by learning a better embedding function."

The loss is the negative log likelihood of the correct passage against a set of wrong ones, and the trick that makes it cheap is that the wrong ones come free. In a batch of B questions and B passages you compute all B by B similarities, the diagonal is your positives, and everything off the diagonal is a negative you didn't have to go find.

So the space is not discovered. It is built, by repeatedly pushing correct pairs together and incorrect pairs apart, and it will only be good at the kinds of relatedness that were in the training pairs.

The reported gains are real and worth stating precisely: DPR "outperforms a strong Lucene-BM25 system largely by 9%-19% absolute in terms of top-20 passage retrieval accuracy", and on Natural Questions specifically, 78.4% against 59.1%.

Those are aggregate results on open-domain question answering. They are not a measurement of our boots. I want to be careful here, because it would be easy to write that the paraphrase problem is now solved and let the earlier example quietly borrow the credit. What the objective is *designed* to do is put `wide toe box` and `roomy forefoot` near each other. Whether it does that for your catalog is a question about your data and your model, and the honest answer is that you'd have to measure it.

Now the second query. `Salomon Quest 4 GTX wide 10.5`, the one term matching was superb at.

A bi-encoder compresses that entire string into 768 floats, and `Quest 4` and `Quest 5` land very close together, because they are textually and semantically almost identical. Nothing in the training objective taught the model that the difference between them is the whole point. [Sciavolino and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.08535) measured this directly, using simple questions about specific named entities, and found that "dense retrievers drastically underperform sparse methods", and, more damningly, that "dense retrievers can only generalize to common entities unless the question pattern is explicitly observed during training."

That is a Wikidata entity study, not a product catalog. But the mechanism transfers cleanly, and if you have ever watched a semantic search return the wrong model number with great confidence, you have seen it.

And `under $150` is exactly where it was. The vector space has no notion of a price constraint. We have traded one of our two failures for a different one, and left the other untouched.

### 7. Why not a tree, and what a graph does instead

You have a hundred million vectors and a query vector. The obvious move is a tree: cut the space into regions, descend into the right one, ignore the rest.

This does not work, and the reason is a formal result rather than a folk observation. Weber, Schek and Blott proved it in [1998](https://www.vldb.org/conf/1998/p194.pdf): "We show formally that these methods exhibit linear complexity at high dimensionality, and that existing methods are outperformed on average by a simple sequential scan if the number of dimensions exceeds around 10." And more sweepingly: "There is no organization of HDVS based on partitioning or clustering which does not degenerate to a sequential scan if dimensionality exceeds a certain threshold."

Ten dimensions. We have 768.

What works instead is a graph you walk. Link each vector to some of its neighbours, start somewhere, and repeatedly step to whichever neighbour is closer to the query until none of them is. You are not pruning a search space. You are navigating one.

[HNSW](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.09320) is the version everyone runs, and it has two ideas, only one of which is the one it's famous for.

The famous one is the layers. Higher layers hold a shrinking sample of the points, so you enter at the top, take huge steps across a nearly empty graph, drop down, refine, and drop again. It is a skip list generalized to a metric space, and the paper says so in as many words: the layer-assignment parameter is `1/ln(M)`, and "this corresponds to the skip list parameter p=1/M".

The other idea is the one that does more work, and it's in how neighbours get chosen. If you connect each node to its M nearest neighbours, you get a graph that can't route: in a cluster, all your links point into the cluster, and a walk that enters gets stuck. So HNSW keeps a candidate only if it's closer to the new node than to any neighbour already selected. The result approximates a known object, "the exact relative neighborhood graph, a minimal subgraph of the Delaunay graph deducible by using only the distances between the nodes", and the property it buys is that your links point in different directions. The paper's own phrasing is "connections in diverse directions". Without it, "the Hierarchical NSW algorithm fails to achieve a high recall for clustered data because the search stucks at the clusters boundaries."

{/* TODO: Fig 6: HnswAnatomy. Panels: skip-list layers; the pruning heuristic choosing diverse directions vs nearest; the three degree numbers side by side. */}

Which of the two matters more? The authors answer this themselves, about real data, and it is not the answer their algorithm's name suggests: on SIFT vectors, "the performance improvement by increasing the mL is higher, but less prominent at current settings compared to improvement from the heuristic."

In 2024 someone finished that sentence. Munyampirwa, Lakshman and Coleman [removed the hierarchy entirely](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01940) and found "a flat navigable small world graph graph retains all of the benefits of HNSW on high-dimensional datasets, with latency and recall performance essentially identical to the original algorithm but with less memory overhead", saving roughly 38% and 39% of peak construction memory on two Big-ANN datasets. Their explanation is that hub nodes emerge naturally and do the long-range routing the layers were credited with. They are also honest that they don't fully know why: "Perhaps most importantly, we still have no satisfactory understanding of why hierarchy does not help."

Now the numbers, because three different ones get quoted interchangeably and they are not the same number.

`M` is the parameter you set, typically 16. The base layer allocates twice that: "2∙M is a good choice for Mmax0", so 32 slots per node. Averaged across all layers the paper's own formula gives about 37.8 link slots per element, which at four bytes each is roughly 151 bytes of connection-ID capacity per vector. That formula reproduces the paper's stated range of "about 60-450 bytes per object" at both ends, so it's trustworthy.

But allocated capacity is not realized degree. The pruning heuristic rejects candidates, and most nodes never fill their slots. Qdrant measured their own one-million-point collection and reported that "at Qdrant's default m=16, the one-million-point collection benchmarked below averaged about 21 links per node on layer 0."

Thirty-two allocated. Thirty-eight slots on average across layers. Twenty-one actually used, on one measured dataset. When someone quotes a degree number at you, ask which one they mean, because the next section turns on it.

### 8. What a filter does to a graph

We still have `under $150` sitting in the query, unaddressed since section 5. It is time to put it into the index, and it is going to cause trouble.

There are three places to apply it, and the [VLDB tutorial](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol18/p5488-caminal.pdf) on filtered vector search names them cleanly. Filter first, then search the survivors. Search first, then discard the misses. Or filter during the walk. The first is optimal when the surviving set is small. The second has a cost the tutorial states directly: it "requires the approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) search to yield a multiple of K results to ensure at least K vectors remain after filtering, which complicates achieving high recall." Want ten results from a filter that keeps 1% of the corpus, and you need roughly a thousand candidates before filtering. Want them from a filter keeping 0.1%, and you are approaching a scan with extra steps.

The third option is the one everyone wants, and it's where the graph turns on you.

Here is the shape of the problem, stated carefully, because it is easy to state carelessly. Your walk can only step to neighbours that pass the filter. Nodes that fail are dead ends. As the filter gets more selective, each node's usable neighbourhood thins out, and at some point the walk stops being able to reach the region of the space where the answers are.

You will see this described as percolation, with a threshold where a graph of degree `d` shatters once a filter keeps less than `1/d` of the nodes. I had that in an earlier draft of this post and took it out, because it does not survive contact with the actual objects. Percolation thresholds of that form are results about randomly wired graphs. An HNSW graph is geometric, deliberately pruned toward directional diversity, and full of hub nodes. And more importantly, a real filter is not random node removal. `under $150` is correlated with position in the embedding space, because cheap boots and expensive boots do not describe themselves identically. The whole derivation assumes away the thing that actually happens.

The [ACORN paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.04871) gives the version you can actually defend. For a predicate with selectivity `s`, the expected surviving degree of a node is its degree times `s`, and the concern is stated plainly: "If a node in the predicate subgraph has degree much lower than M, this could adversely impact the search convergence and thus recall." They also bound the probability that a walk strands entirely, at `O(log n · (1-s)^(M·γ))`.

Both results come with an assumption printed right next to them: they hold "For a dataset and query predicate that exhibit no predicate clustering", which is to say, for exactly the uncorrelated case that real filters violate. The paper is candid about how far any of this goes: "We note that neither HNSW nor ACORN provides theoretical guarantees on connectivity over its level graphs for arbitrary datasets. Thus we instead rely primarily on empirical results for our analysis."

So nobody has the theorem. What everyone has is measurements, and they are ugly.

Qdrant published a [benchmark](https://qdrant.tech/articles/filtered-vector-search-acorn/) on a million-point collection, and the plain graph's recall against filter selectivity reads as follows. At a filter keeping 20% of the collection, 62.9%. At 10%, 20.6%. At 1%, 0.1%.

Look at the latency column in that table and it barely moves: 1.6ms, 1.7ms, 1.6ms. The system is exactly as fast while being completely wrong. There is no timeout, no error, no degradation you would notice on a dashboard. It returns ten confident results that are not the answer.

That failure mode is worse than being slow, and it is why this subfield exists.

Two repairs, and they differ in when they do the work.

ACORN repairs at query time. When a neighbour fails the predicate, don't stop; look through it to its neighbours. The predicate check is a bitmap lookup rather than a distance computation, so you can filter the expanded list before spending arithmetic on it. There are two variants, and the names only appear in the body of the paper, not the abstract: ACORN-γ builds a denser graph up front by collecting `M · γ` candidate edges per node, and ACORN-1 does the expansion purely at search time. The tradeoff between them is stated as "at most 5× lower QPS at fixed recall but 9–53× lower TTI".

Qdrant repairs at build time. Declare a payload field indexed and they additionally link the points that share each value, so the `brand = Salomon` subgraph is connected because somebody built a graph over exactly those points. It costs: "the HNSW index built in 116 seconds without them and 507 to 652 seconds with them, 4.4x to 5.6x the cost."

Neither wins everywhere, and the axis is not the one you'd guess. It is not selectivity. It is whether the filter is a single field or an intersection of two.

On a single field at 1% selectivity, the extra edges win handily: 99.8% recall at 1.0ms against ACORN's 67.7% at 4.7ms. Build-time repair, applied to exactly the field being filtered, is hard to beat.

On a two-field AND at 4%, it reverses: ACORN 95.2% at 7.7ms, extra edges 63.7% at 1.2ms. And the reason is structural rather than incidental. Qdrant say it themselves: they build those edges "per payload field, never per combination, so an AND filter lands on an intersection that no single field's edges cover." You cannot pre-build edges for every combination of fields, because the number of combinations is exponential in the number of fields.

The system that actually ships doesn't pick. Qdrant's planner estimates the filter's cardinality first and routes: tiny result sets skip the graph and scan the matching IDs directly, broad filters take ordinary traversal, and the middle gets the repair. With all of it engaged their default configuration holds 100% recall on every filter shape they tested, single and compound, at latencies between 1.2 and 7.3ms.

Which is the real lesson, and it generalizes past any one vendor: filtered vector search is a query planner with two strategies, not an algorithm.

### 9. What the repairs cost, and where the vector ends up living

Every fix in the last section made the index bigger.

Extra edges cost 4.4 to 5.6 times the build time and the storage that goes with them. ACORN-γ collects `M · γ` candidate edges per node instead of `M`, and the paper's suggested γ is roughly `1/s_min`, so serving 1% filters means an order of magnitude more candidate edges. And before any of that, HNSW was already charging you about 151 bytes of connection-ID capacity per vector at M=16.

So the index you just repaired is the one that stops fitting. That is what forces the next set of decisions, and they are the ones that decide your bill.

Start with the vector itself. A 768-dimension float32 vector is 3,072 bytes. A hundred million of them is 307 GB before you have stored a single graph edge.

Product quantization cuts it by chopping the vector into subvectors and replacing each with a codebook index. The arithmetic is simple: M subquantizers of K centroids each costs `M · ceil(log2 K)` bits, so 96 subvectors at 8 bits is 96 bytes, a 32-fold reduction. DiskANN uses "32 bytes per data point" and gets a billion vectors onto one machine.

Binary quantization goes further, and until recently it went further than it could justify. [RaBitQ](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.12497) changed that by giving the approach an error bound, noting that prior methods "do not have a theoretical error bound and are observed to fail disastrously on some real-world datasets."

But the part that makes binary quantization work in production is not the quantization. It's what you do afterward. Elastic, who ship it, are blunt about the naive version: "Naive binary quantization is exceptionally lossy and achieving adequate recall requires gathering 10x or 100x additional neighbors to rerank. This just doesn't cut it." Their implementation gets "above 90% recall with only 3x oversampling", and the oversampled candidates get rescored against the original float32 vectors. The compressed index finds candidates. The uncompressed vectors decide the order. If you take one operational thing from this section, take that.

Truncation is the cheapest lever and the most misunderstood. You can cut dimensions off the end of an embedding and it mostly still works, and you do not need a model specially trained for it. A [2026 study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.16608) tested exactly this and found that "truncated embeddings of non-MRL models are competitive with, and often outperform models trained with MRL" unless you cut by at least 80%. Matryoshka training buys you the heavy-truncation case and not much else.

Note that the threshold is a percentage, not a dimension. Going from 1024 to 256 is a 75% cut and inside the safe zone. Going from 1536 to 256 is 83% and is not. "Truncate to 256" is not advice; it depends entirely on where you started.

{/* TODO: Fig 8: VectorResidence. One vector's footprint as a stacked bar (raw, connection-ID capacity, filter-repair overhead) at three compression settings; second panel places those footprints against RAM, SSD and object-storage capacity and latency. */}

Now the tier. RAM is the default and the reason vector search has a reputation for being expensive. SSD is what DiskANN is for. And since about 2024 there is a third option that is more interesting than it looks.

turbopuffer runs vector indexes on object storage, and publishes what that costs: "The first query to a namespace reads object storage directly and is slow (p50=874ms for 1M documents), but subsequent, cached queries to that node are faster (p50=14ms for 1M documents)." Sixty-fold, cold to warm, and the cold path is what it is because "each roundtrip to object storage takes ~100ms."

Here is the part nobody frames as what it is. They are not running a graph. Their index is "based on SPFresh", a centroid-based design, and the stated reason is that a centroid index "works well for object storage as it minimizes roundtrips and write-amplification, compared to graph-based indexes like HNSW or DiskANN."

Graph traversal is a sequence of dependent lookups: you cannot know your third hop until you've made your second. That is fine at RAM latency and fatal at 100ms per roundtrip. So moving the index to object storage did not just change where the bytes live. In this case it changed the family of index that makes sense, back toward the centroid approach that graphs had displaced. Not a universal law about storage tiers, but a documented case where the tier chose the algorithm.

## Act 4 — The reconvergence

### 10. What you write into the index

For most of this post the two camps have looked like opposites. Terms on one side, vectors on the other. That framing is about to stop working, and the thing that breaks it is unglamorous: people started using neural models to decide what goes into an inverted index.

The framing that makes it click is [Lin and Ma's](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.14807): sparse against dense is one axis, and unsupervised against learned is a different one. Nothing says a sparse representation has to be produced by counting words. You can learn the term weights.

That is what SPLADE does, and the punchline is in the implementation note rather than the method: their code "is built on the Lucene search library and thus fully compatible with standard inverted indexes." The model runs, produces weights, and writes them into postings. Everything downstream, the skipping from section 4 included, works unchanged.

{/* TODO: Fig 9: LearnedPostings. The Fig 2 posting list holding learned term weights, beside the BM25 version. */}

[SPLADE-v3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06789) reports that "it is statistically significantly more effective than both BM25 and SPLADE++", getting "more than 40 MRR@10 on the MS MARCO dev set" and improving BEIR out-of-domain results by 2%.

Then comes the part that inverts everyone's intuition about where the cost is.

You would assume the expensive bit is the neural network. It isn't. Query expansion turns a five-term query into a hundred-term query, and traversing an inverted index gets more expensive with more query terms. The [efficiency study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.03834) says it directly: the main gain came from "the reduction of SPLADE query sizes, instead of focusing solely on the FLOPS measure", because "query size is then a major bottleneck." The neural half was cheap. The 1970s half was what blew up.

The same lesson shows up on the document side, and harder. doc2query generates queries a document might answer and appends them before indexing. It works. It also hallucinates. Gospodinov, MacAvaney and Macdonald [filtered the generated text](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.03266) with a relevance model and found you can "improve the retrieval effectiveness of Doc2Query by up to 16%, while simultaneously reducing mean query execution time by 23% and cutting the index size by 33%." That is from keeping only 30% of what the model generated. Throwing away seven-tenths of your generated text made retrieval better, the index smaller, and queries faster, all at once.

Which raises the question this whole section is really about: what is the unit you index?

For a product catalog it's obvious, one document per boot. For a 200-page PDF it isn't, and this is the most-asked practical question in retrieval. The published evidence is thinner than the volume of advice suggests, but 2026 finally produced controlled sweeps, and one of them killed a piece of folklore outright.

The [chunking study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.14123) varied method, size, overlap and context budget on Natural Questions with SPLADE retrieval and a Mistral-8B generator. On overlap, the standard advice for years has been 10 to 20%. Their finding: "overlap provides no measurable benefit and increases indexing cost." It also found "sentence chunking is the most cost-effective method, matching semantic chunking up to ~5k tokens", and a ceiling: "a 'context cliff' reduces quality beyond ~2.5k tokens."

There is an apparent contradiction in this literature worth resolving, because you will run into both papers. One study concludes semantic chunking isn't worth its cost. Another concludes content-aware chunking beats fixed-size chunking decisively. They are not in conflict; they define the baseline differently. The first compares semantic chunking against a baseline that already splits on sentence boundaries with overlap. The second compares against fixed-size splitting by *character*, which cuts words in half. Both are consistent with one reading: respect sentence and paragraph boundaries, and don't pay extra for embedding-similarity segmentation on top of that.

One caution on the second study, and it matters more than it looks. Its relevance judgments come from "a state-of-the-art LLM evaluator" with no human anchor. Hold that thought; section 19 is about exactly what that costs you.

### 11. Two lists, one order

You now have two retrievers with different failure modes. Lexical is superb on `Salomon Quest 4 GTX wide 10.5` and helpless on `wide toe box`. Dense is the reverse. Running both is obvious. Combining their output is not.

The problem is that their scores are not comparable. BM25 produces an unbounded sum whose scale depends on your corpus statistics. Cosine similarity lives in [-1, 1]. Adding them is meaningless, and normalizing them requires knowing something about each distribution that you often don't.

Reciprocal rank fusion sidesteps all of it by throwing the scores away and keeping only the ranks. Each document scores `1/(k + rank)` in each list, and you sum. No calibration, no distributions, nothing to tune.

Almost nothing to tune. There is `k`, and it is set to 60 essentially everywhere. That number comes from [Cormack, Clarke and Buettcher in 2009](https://cormack.uwaterloo.ca/cormacksigir09-rrf.pdf), and they are candid about its provenance: "k = 60 was fixed during a pilot investigation and not altered during subsequent validation." Their pilot "indicated that k = 60 was near-optimal, but that the choice was not critical."

So it was chosen, on TREC data, in 2009, using a search engine most people reading this have never run. Elasticsearch ships it as `rank_constant`, defaulting to 60, and sells the method on exactly the property that made it attractive then: "RRF requires no tuning, and the different relevance indicators do not have to be related to each other to achieve high-quality results."

Then someone checked.

Bruch, Gai and Ingber [analysed fusion functions properly](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.11934) and their abstract opens with the word "Contrary": "we find RRF to be sensitive to its parameters; that the learning of a CC fusion is generally agnostic to the choice of score normalization; that CC outperforms RRF in in-domain and out-of-domain settings; and finally, that CC is sample efficient, requiring only a small set of training examples to tune its only parameter."

Every clause there cuts against the received wisdom. The method sold as needing no tuning is the parameter-sensitive one. The normalization problem that rank-based fusion exists to avoid turns out not to matter much. And a plain weighted combination of scores wins, in and out of domain, and needs only a few hundred labelled examples to fit its single parameter.

A 2026 [benchmark on 23,088 financial queries](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01733) reran the comparison and got the same direction: "CC with α=0.5 (equal weighting of BM25 and dense scores) achieves Recall@5 of 0.726, outperforming RRF (k=60) at 0.695." Note what that does and does not say. Equal weighting beat default RRF on that benchmark. It does not say 0.5 is the right weight for your corpus; it says the untuned linear thing beat the untuned rank thing.

What should you actually expect from hybrid? Less than the enthusiasm suggests, and that is fine, because the value isn't in the average. A [SIGIR 2026 reproducibility study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.02558) tested a promising lexical variant across benchmarks and found "its gains largely specific to BRIGHT, while fusion with standard BM25 provides the most consistent improvements across datasets." Fusion buys consistency across corpora rather than a large win on any one of them. If you are hoping hybrid will add five points, it probably won't. If you are hoping it will stop you falling off a cliff on the corpus you didn't anticipate, that is the documented benefit.

That same study is worth one more paragraph, because it is a small scandal. In reproducing the benchmark's own published lexical baseline, the authors found they couldn't, until they identified "a key under-documented detail: query-side BM25 (BM25Q), which applies BM25 weighting to the query itself." They also audited the corpus and uncovered "data quality issues that impact evaluation."

A number in a paper is not the same object as a reproducible result. Keep that handy; it comes back.
