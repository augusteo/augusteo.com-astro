*A mechanism-first take on Julia Galef's 2021 book* The Scout Mindset. *Not a book review. The post walks the empirical literature on motivated reasoning, identity-protective cognition, and the Good Judgment Project, then prescribes the smallest durable habit consistent with what the sources actually say. About a 30-minute read.*

## Act 1 — The puzzle

### 1. The smallest interesting case: one belief, two updates

Take a belief you actually hold. Something you'd defend in a conversation, with numbers if pressed. Anything contested enough that smart people disagree, where you've already thought about it more than the average person you'd argue with.

Now imagine the same piece of new evidence reaching your inbox twice. Once with the labels stripped off, presented as a contingency table about something neutral, like the effectiveness of a skin cream. Once with the same numbers but with the labels of your political tribe attached, presented as evidence about, say, a gun-control policy or a climate intervention. The numbers are identical. The arithmetic is identical. The conclusion the numbers point to is identical. Your update is not.

{/* TODO: Fig 1: OneBeliefTwoUpdates. Same evidence, two framings; priors and posteriors converge in the neutral framing and diverge in the identity-loaded framing. */}

This is the sketch of a result the literature has been arguing about for a decade. Section 3 returns to it in its less stylized form, with the dissents attached. Briefly, for now: in a 2017 study, [Dan Kahan and colleagues](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2319992) reported that, on a politically loaded contingency table, more numerate subjects polarized *more* than less numerate ones, not less. Quantitative skill, deployed in service of an identity, gave you a sharper wrong answer. The strong version of that finding is contested in three different ways and section 3 will lay them out. The smaller claim, that the same brain runs different update procedures when the evidence carries identity tags, survives a wider and older literature that section 3 will also catalogue.

Two things follow from the small case. First, smart-and-careful isn't a defense. The failure mode lives in the architecture of cognition, not in how much effort you brought to the page. Section 3 walks the three settled asymmetries that pin this down empirically, then introduces Kahan's contested fourth case. Second, one person's update on a single piece of evidence is a noisy estimate of the truth. Many people's calibrated updates, aggregated under specific conditions, are a tighter estimate. Section 6 returns to that aggregation insight with the empirical result that anchors the rest of this post.

For now, pick the belief. We'll keep coming back to it.

### 2. The result that shouldn't be possible

Now zoom out. Hold the personal-scale failure mode in mind, then look at what happened when the U.S. intelligence community ran the test at scale.

Between 2011 and 2015, the Intelligence Advanced Research Projects Activity (IARPA) ran a forecasting tournament called the Aggregative Contingent Estimation program. Among the contestants was the [Good Judgment Project](https://goodjudgment.com/), an open team led by Phil Tetlock and Barbara Mellers. Among the parallel benchmarks was a market the U.S. intelligence community ran on the side called the Intelligence Community Prediction Market, populated by analysts with access to classified information. *(The "intelligence analysts with classified-information access" framing for the ICPM is sourced to Tetlock's public characterization of the comparison and to Goldstein's introduction; if a Phase 7 verbatim pull narrows the description, the prose narrows with it.)*

A subsequent technical paper by [Goldstein, Hartman, Comstock and colleagues](https://goodjudgment.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/Goldstein-et-al-GJP-vs-ICPM.pdf) compared GJP aggregation methods to the ICPM on a one-year unclassified-question subset where both populations had answered the same questions. The headline result, scored on Brier (a squared-error metric where lower is better, scaled here on the 0-to-2 range the Goldstein paper uses):

{/* TODO: Fig 2: GJPvsICPM. Two-bar chart, ICPM ≈ 0.23 vs GJP "All Surveys Logit" ≈ 0.15 on 0-to-2 Brier scale; ex-post-selection caveat annotated; subtitle clarifies the 0-to-2 convention. */}

ICPM, the classified-information-access market: about 0.23. GJP's best aggregation method, an "All Surveys Logit" weighting that combined open-tournament forecasters' probabilistic estimates: about 0.15.

That's the puzzle. An open tournament's calibrated-aggregation procedure beat a market populated by professionals with access to classified information.

The way this result usually gets told in popular accounts is something like "elite GJP teams beat the CIA by 25 to 30 percent". That phrasing collapses two findings that need to be kept apart. Goldstein's paper is a comparison of *aggregation methods*, with a clearly-stated caveat that the "best method" was selected ex post from a set of candidates the GJP team built. It isn't a claim that any individual GJP forecaster was sharper than any individual analyst, nor that the GJP team's training package caused the gap. We'll keep those questions separate; the second one is contested in section 6 in ways popular accounts haven't picked up.

What the result *does* say, even after those caveats, is that a calibrated probabilistic aggregation procedure run on open-tournament forecasters could outperform a single prediction market run on classified intelligence. The interesting question is then not "are amateurs better than experts" but "what does the system that won actually do, and which of its properties carry over to a single person who isn't running a tournament?" That's the rest of this post.

A historical note before the section closes. In 1876, late in his life, Charles Darwin wrote a short autobiography for his children. In it he described a single habit he claimed had shaped most of his work, a "golden rule" he had followed for many years. Section 4 quotes it in full. For now: 130 years before IARPA paid people to forecast, an English naturalist running an experiment on himself was already implementing a piece of what won.

### 3. The three asymmetries (and the contested fourth case)

The puzzle from section 2 has a long empirical pre-history. Three pieces of motivated-reasoning research from the 1970s, 1990s, and 2010s converge on the same picture: the brain handles evidence asymmetrically depending on whether the conclusion the evidence points to is one you'd already prefer. The book that put the case in front of a wide audience, [Michael Lewis's *The Undoing Project*](/blog/undoing-project) on Kahneman and Tversky's bias catalog, is the foundation Galef's frame stacks on. This section reaches behind that catalog to three of the specific empirical findings that make the catalog more than a metaphor, and then adds a fourth case the field is still arguing about.

**(a) Directional goals bias what your search retrieves.** Ziva Kunda's 1990 review article "[The Case for Motivated Reasoning](https://fbaum.unc.edu/teaching/articles/Psych-Bulletin-1990-Kunda.pdf)" is the canonical statement of the result. In Kunda's formulation, "directional goals affect reasoning by affecting which information will be considered in the reasoning process." The point is sharper than "smart people rationalize away inconvenient facts." It's that the brain doesn't *find* the inconvenient facts in the first place. Memory retrieval is biased; counterexamples that would surface if the directional goal were different stay below threshold. The reasoning that follows looks careful, careful inferences from a coherent body of evidence, and it is careful; it just operates on a curated input.

**(b) Identical evidence polarizes, not converges.** The 1979 Lord, Ross, and Lepper study put 48 Stanford undergraduates with strong prior beliefs about capital punishment in front of a deliberately balanced packet of two studies, one supporting and one undermining the deterrence hypothesis. Both groups read the same studies. Both groups walked away more confident in their original positions than they had been before the experiment started. In the authors' words, "the result of exposing contending factions in a social dispute to an identical body of relevant empirical evidence may be not a narrowing of disagreement but rather an increase in polarization." Same evidence in. Greater disagreement out.

**(c) Preferred conclusions face lower evidentiary thresholds.** Peter Ditto and David Lopez, in 1992, gave subjects a fake medical test for a fake "TAA enzyme" deficiency. Half the subjects were told the result they got indicated a serious health problem; the other half were told their result indicated they were fine. Subjects in the unfavorable-result condition spent longer scrutinizing the test, were more likely to retest, and were more likely to question the test's validity. In Ditto and Lopez's words: "information consistent with a preferred conclusion is examined less critically than information inconsistent with a preferred conclusion, and consequently, less information is required to reach the former than the latter." The bar moves with the conclusion.

These three are settled in the sense that they replicate, generalize across domains, and find their way into review articles a generation later. Take them together and the personal-scale failure mode from section 1 looks less like a quirk and more like a default operating procedure: the brain retrieves selectively (Kunda), polarizes under identical evidence (Lord/Ross/Lepper), and applies a lower bar to conclusions you already prefer (Ditto/Lopez).

Panels a-c illustrate the three settled asymmetries (sources Kunda 1990, LRL 1979, D&L 1992); panel d is visually distinguished, marked "contested fourth case", and includes a footnote box with the Persson/Connor/Glüer-Pagin & Spectre dissents. */}

**(d) The contested fourth case: identity-protective cognition.** Dan Kahan's research program is the most recent addition to this picture. His [2017 paper "Motivated Numeracy and Enlightened Self-Government"](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2319992), with Peters, Dawson, and Slovic, took the Lord/Ross/Lepper-style polarization result and asked whether quantitative skill rescues you from it. The answer the paper reports: no, and worse than no. On a politically loaded contingency table about gun-control policy, more numerate subjects polarized along their existing political identity *more* than less numerate ones did, not less. Kahan's frame for this is *identity-protective cognition*: under the right conditions, the brain deploys whatever cognitive equipment it has, including numeracy, in service of conclusions consistent with the identity it's defending. The default-to-truth adaptation [Malcolm Gladwell describes in *Talking to Strangers*](/blog/talking-to-strangers), where the brain assumes the speaker isn't lying, is one of these sensible-but-narrow adaptations; default-to-defend-identity is another.

If that result held cleanly, it would be the most striking asymmetry of the four. It hasn't held cleanly. [Persson and colleagues' 2021 preregistered replication](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2021.104768) in *Cognition* "did not find good evidence for motivated numeracy" under preregistered criteria. [Connor and colleagues' replication in a Western European sample](https://doi.org/10.1017/bpp.2020.32) "replicate the basic motivated numeracy effect, but...do not find evidence of increased polarization among high-numeracy participants." That is, the small effect survived; the dramatic high-numeracy amplification didn't. [Glüer-Pagin and Spectre's 2024 conceptual critique](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13164-024-00737-w) argues the mechanism Kahan posits doesn't quite fit the data: the effect "involve[s] plenty of motivation to reason, but no motivated reasoning."

So the post takes the broader idea, that quantitative skill can be deployed in service of identity and the architecture of cognition supports this kind of use, as the candidate mechanism. It explicitly hedges the specific high-numeracy-amplifies-polarization dose response. We're calling this the contested fourth case rather than the fourth asymmetry on purpose. It's a different kind of finding than (a) through (c): more recent, more disputed, more central to a particular political-psychology research program. The intuition payoff is sharper for being honest. Smart people fail at calibrated judgment because the architecture of cognition is built around three settled asymmetries, plus a fourth case the field is still arguing about.

### 4. The architecture beneath: why the asymmetries are the default

The three settled asymmetries from section 3 don't behave like bugs. They don't go away with more education, more careful thinking, or better intentions. Two research programs from the past two decades argue that the reason is structural: the asymmetries are surface features of an architecture that was selected for, not bolted on after the fact.

**Reason is built for argument.** Hugo Mercier and Dan Sperber's *The Enigma of Reason* (Harvard University Press, 2017) is the most-discussed recent statement of the case. Their argumentative theory holds that reasoning evolved primarily to produce and evaluate arguments in social-persuasive contexts, not to help the individual arrive at better beliefs in isolation. If that's the design brief, the asymmetries from section 3 stop looking like flaws. Confirmation bias starts looking like a working feature: it's how you build a strong case for your side. Polarization on identical evidence is what you'd expect from a system that's optimizing for "win the argument," not "converge on truth."

**Self-deception serves outward deception.** Robert Trivers's *The Folly of Fools* (Basic Books, 2011) is the older complement. Trivers's central argument is that self-deception evolved as a mechanism that makes outward deception more credible. If the false belief is genuinely held, the deceiver transmits no micro-expressions, no autonomic signals. The brain has to be its own audience first; the listener is the second audience. The line that recurs in Trivers's interviews about the book is that the primary reason we fool ourselves is to fool others, and the reason we fool others is the same reason any social organism deceives: there's an information advantage on the line.

Take Mercier-Sperber and Trivers together and the asymmetries from section 3 stop looking like a personal failure. Soldier mode is the architecture, working as designed, on the design brief it was selected for. The brain you're walking around with was built to win arguments and to deceive credibly. It happens to also be the brain you're now asking to estimate probabilities about geopolitical events.

This has a stronger consequence than "try harder to think well." It says a single reader, sitting alone, running self-tests on themselves, can only get so far against an architecture that's social. Section 7 will give you five of those self-tests anyway, and the literature says they help. But section 8 will insist that the architecture is the load-bearing problem and that the durable counter-program needs a *systems* layer too. The diagnosis Mercier-Sperber and Trivers offer is what makes that demand non-optional.

The historical point of contact is Darwin. In the autobiography he wrote in his last years, [published posthumously in 1887 by his son Francis](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2010/pg2010-images.html), Darwin gave one paragraph to a single working habit:

> "I had, also, during many years, followed a golden rule, namely, that whenever a published fact, a new observation or thought came across me, which was opposed to my general results, to make a memorandum of it without fail and at once; for I had found by experience that such facts and thoughts were far more apt to escape from memory than favourable ones. Owing to this habit, very few objections were raised against my views which I had not at least noticed and attempted to answer."

Read it twice. Darwin's contribution to the scout-mode toolkit isn't a thought experiment. It's an *artifact*. The notebook is an external system that catches the disconfirming observations the directional-goal-biased retrieval mechanism in Kunda's 1990 description would otherwise let drop. Run the architecture as designed, with no countermeasures, and Kunda's selection effect chews through the inconvenient evidence in days. Run the architecture *plus the notebook*, and the inconvenient evidence is on a timestamped line that's still legible six years later, when you're writing chapter twelve. Different game.

Section 8 returns to the artifact idea and generalizes it. The Darwin notebook is the personal-scale instance of a class of moves Galef calls operational habits and the GJP research program calls calibration tracking. Section 10 closes the post on the same notebook.

## Act 2 — The scout's measurable side

### 5. AOMT, Brier, and what calibration actually looks like

We left section 4 with a diagnosis (soldier mode is the architecture, working as designed) and a single counter-move (Darwin's notebook, an external system). Act 2 stops feeling the problem and starts measuring it. Two ideas, one section: the dispositional trait the GJP literature uses to talk about scouts, and the metric that lets "calibrated" be a number rather than a vibe.

**Actively open-minded thinking.** The trait has a name and an instrument. Jonathan Baron's textbook *Thinking and Deciding* (4th edition, Cambridge University Press, 2008) defines actively open-minded thinking, AOMT, as the willingness to seek contrary evidence, to weigh new evidence against held beliefs, to tolerate complexity, and to update in proportion to evidence. The first published instrument that operationalizes the construct as a survey scale is Stanovich and West's 1997 paper. The construct is a research operationalization of what Galef calls the scout orientation: not a temperamental trait you're born with, not "intellectual humility" as a virtue, but a measurable propensity to do specific things in the presence of disconfirming information.

In the Good Judgment Project, AOMT was *a* predictor of individual forecaster accuracy. The 2015 *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied* paper by [Mellers, Stone, Atanasov and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1037/xap0000040) lists it alongside several others, in the authors' own framing: "key predictors of accuracy were dispositional variables of cognitive ability, political knowledge, and open-mindedness; situational variables of training in probabilistic reasoning and participation in collaborative teams that shared information and discussed rationales; and behavioral variables of deliberation time and frequency of belief updating." That's a list of seven things. AOMT is one. It isn't "the strongest" predictor and it isn't single-handedly responsible for the GJP's results. The post will be careful to keep that framing because the literature does. What AOMT *is* is one trait you could plausibly cultivate, that the literature has measurably correlated with one outcome you'd plausibly want.

**Brier score.** The metric. For a binary forecast (the event happens or it doesn't), Brier score is the mean squared error between the probability you assigned and the outcome. If you said 0.7 and the event happened (outcome = 1), your error on that forecast is $(0.7 - 1)^2 = 0.09$. If you said 0.7 and it didn't happen (outcome = 0), your error is $(0.7)^2 = 0.49$. Average those squared errors over a long sequence of forecasts and you have your Brier score. Lower is better. Zero is perfect.

A convention note that matters because it bites later. [Glenn Brier's 1950 original paper](https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/mwre/78/1/1520-0493_1950_078_0001_vofeit_2_0_co_2.xml) defines the score on a 0-to-2 range for two-outcome forecasts. The Goldstein-vs-ICPM paper from section 2 reports its numbers on that same 0-to-2 scale; the ICPM ≈ 0.23 and GJP-best ≈ 0.15 sit on that range. Modern individual-forecaster Brier in the Mellers 2014 and 2015 GJP papers is reported on the 0-to-1 scale you get from binary squared error. They're not different metrics; they're two scalings. The post says explicitly which scale is in play whenever a number appears. The 0.08 Brier gap from section 2 is on the 0-to-2 scale. That's a gap big enough to matter and small enough that you wouldn't bet your career on a single forecaster outperforming a single market.

A calibration plot is the picture that ties AOMT and Brier together. Pool a forecaster's predictions by the probability they assigned (everything in the 0.7-bucket, everything in the 0.8-bucket), then ask what fraction of those events actually happened. A forecaster who said 70 percent and was right 70 percent of the time sits on the diagonal at the 0.7 mark. A forecaster who said 90 percent and was right 60 percent of the time is overconfident; on the plot, their curve sags below the diagonal at the high-probability end. Brier score collapses the whole plot to one number; the calibration plot is what the number comes from. AOMT is a candidate trait that nudges the curve toward the diagonal. [Nassim Taleb's *Fooled by Randomness*](/blog/fooled-by-randomness) made the case fifteen years before the GJP did that the human mind isn't built for sophisticated probability; the calibration plot is the thing you measure when you decide to build the muscle anyway.

You can do this on yourself. The obvious caveat: a small number of forecasts gives a noisy curve, and most personal calibration tracking lives in the small-N regime. Section 8 returns to the personal-scale version of this practice in detail. The point in section 5 is that "I'm well-calibrated" stops being a claim about character once you can show the plot. It becomes a claim with a number attached, scored on a metric that's defined on a finite range, that someone else can reproduce from the same forecasts and outcomes.

### 6. The aggregator beats experts: Goldstein revisited, and the Hauenstein open question

Section 5 gave us the trait (AOMT) and the metric (Brier). With those in hand we can return to the puzzle from section 2 and ask the question we couldn't ask cleanly back then: what does the aggregation procedure that beat the ICPM actually do?

The honest answer is that the post can sketch the *kind* of thing it does qualitatively, and can't make a load-bearing primary-source claim about the exact arithmetic without pulling more weight from the GJP team's technical documentation than is reasonable. The high-level shape: aggregating many probabilistic forecasts can reduce variance, *under specific assumptions about how the forecasters relate to each other.* The two assumptions that carry the most weight are (a) independence between forecasters and (b) errors that aren't too strongly correlated. If both hold, the central limit theorem does most of the work; the average of ten forecasters is a tighter estimate than any one of them. If correlation is tight, you're basically looking at one forecaster ten times, and the noise you wanted to wash out doesn't wash out.

Picture it as a funnel. One forecaster's distribution of estimates is wide and possibly off-center. That's panel (i). Ten independent forecasters with the same bias, averaged naively, give a narrower distribution centered on the same off-center mean. Variance shrinks; bias doesn't move. That's panel (ii). Ten *correlated* forecasters with the same bias, averaged the same way, give a distribution that's narrower than panel (i) but wider than panel (ii). Correlation eats the variance reduction. That's panel (iii). The funnel doesn't say "aggregation always beats truth." It says "aggregation can reduce variance under independence; that's the mechanism worth a name."

The GJP's "All Surveys Logit" is a more sophisticated procedure than a flat average. The GJP team's published descriptions characterize it as a logit-pooling weighting that gives more weight to recent forecasts and to forecasters with track records, with the underlying mathematical machinery doing the work of damping correlated noise. The post will not put that description on a load-bearing line; if Phase 7 verbatim verification turns up a different aggregation specification, the prose updates with the specification. What matters at the section's level of abstraction is that the procedure is *trying* to be the right-hand panel of the funnel, not the left-hand panel.

The other side of the section-2 comparison, the ICPM, is a prediction market. Prediction markets have known pathologies. They herd; they thin out at expiration; their prices are noisy when liquidity is thin. The post won't assert *which* of those pathologies drove the ICPM's 0.23 Brier, because the Goldstein paper diagnoses the comparison without diagnosing the cause. We have an aggregation-method comparison and an outcome; we don't have a clean primary-source story about why the market lost. That's a finer-grained question the field hasn't published. The post leaves it open.

**Now the open question that matters most.** Section 2 made a careful split: Goldstein's algorithmic-aggregation comparison on one side, the Mellers et al. 2014 *Psychological Science* claims about training, teaming, and tracking on the other. The aggregation result holds. The training-and-teaming causal claim is the part that's contested.

[Hauenstein, Thomas, Illingworth, and Dougherty 2025](https://doi.org/10.1177/09567976241266481) in *Psychological Science* (online December 2024) reanalyzed Mellers's 2014 data using an item-response-theory framework that models forecasters' latent ability from their item-by-item choices. Under that reanalysis, with key extraneous variables controlled, the relationship between training/teaming and forecast accuracy "differed from the interpretation of the original findings." In plain-English terms: the effects shrank, in some specifications vanished, in some reversed sign. That isn't the same thing as "the GJP findings don't replicate." It's that the data are consistent with a smaller causal role for the specific interventions Mellers et al. credited, once you control for method-variance variables that look more like artifacts of the original analysis than like real causes.

No published Mellers or Tetlock response to Hauenstein has surfaced as of this post's publication date. The post's framing is the obvious one: the aggregation result holds (Goldstein); the question of which causal mechanism drove individual-level forecasting gains is open (Hauenstein). One beat further: the literature visibly arguing about itself is itself an instance of scout-mode behavior at the field scale. A research community that lets an IRT-reanalysis paper land in *Psychological Science* and waits to see what the rebuttal looks like is doing the operation section 4 said the architecture wouldn't naturally do alone. Adversarial collaboration at the literature level, which section 8 will return to, is what makes the field self-correcting without requiring any individual to be a saint.

Act 2 closes with two clean things and one open thing. AOMT is measurable. Brier is measurable. Aggregation under independence is a real mechanism with a math story behind it. The Mellers-Hauenstein dispute is the open piece. Act 3 takes both halves into section 7's individual moves and section 8's systems-layer moves.
