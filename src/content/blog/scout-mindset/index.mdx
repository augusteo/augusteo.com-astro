*A mechanism-first take on Julia Galef's 2021 book* The Scout Mindset. *Not a book review. The post walks the empirical literature on motivated reasoning, identity-protective cognition, and the Good Judgment Project, then prescribes the smallest durable habit consistent with what the sources actually say. About a 30-minute read.*

## Act 1 — The puzzle

### 1. The smallest interesting case: one belief, two updates

Take a belief you actually hold. Something you'd defend in a conversation, with numbers if pressed. Anything contested enough that smart people disagree, where you've already thought about it more than the average person you'd argue with.

Now imagine the same piece of new evidence reaching your inbox twice. Once with the labels stripped off, presented as a contingency table about something neutral, like the effectiveness of a skin cream. Once with the same numbers but with the labels of your political tribe attached, presented as evidence about, say, a gun-control policy or a climate intervention. The numbers are identical. The arithmetic is identical. The conclusion the numbers point to is identical. Your update is not.

This is the sketch of a result the literature has been arguing about for a decade. Section 3 returns to it in its less stylized form, with the dissents attached. Briefly, for now: in a 2017 study, [Dan Kahan and colleagues](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2319992) reported that, on a politically loaded contingency table, more numerate subjects polarized *more* than less numerate ones, not less. That high-numeracy amplification is contested in three different ways, and §3 lays them out. The narrower claim, that the same contingency table can produce different readings depending on the labels attached to it, is the part of Kahan's setup that connects to the older biased-evidence-handling literature (Lord, Ross, Lepper 1979; Ditto and Lopez 1992), where prior beliefs shaped how subjects weighed identical material. The post takes the narrower claim seriously while explicitly hedging the dose response.

Two things follow from the small case. First, smart-and-careful isn't a defense. The failure mode lives in the architecture of cognition, not in how much effort you brought to the page. Section 3 walks the three settled asymmetries that pin this down empirically, then introduces Kahan's contested fourth case. Second, one person's update on a single piece of evidence is a noisy estimate of the truth. Many people's calibrated updates, aggregated under specific conditions, are a tighter estimate. Section 6 returns to that aggregation insight with the empirical result that anchors the rest of this post.

For now, pick the belief. We'll keep coming back to it.

### 2. The result that shouldn't be possible

Now zoom out. Hold the personal-scale failure mode in mind, then look at what happened when the U.S. intelligence community ran the test at scale.

Between 2011 and 2015, the Intelligence Advanced Research Projects Activity (IARPA) ran a forecasting tournament called the Aggregative Contingent Estimation program. Among the contestants was the [Good Judgment Project](https://goodjudgment.com/), an open team led by Phil Tetlock and Barbara Mellers. Among the parallel benchmarks was a market the U.S. intelligence community ran on the side called the Intelligence Community Prediction Market.

A subsequent technical paper by [Goldstein, Hartman, Comstock and colleagues](https://goodjudgment.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/Goldstein-et-al-GJP-vs-ICPM.pdf) compared GJP aggregation methods to the ICPM on a one-year unclassified-question subset where both populations had answered the same questions. The headline result, scored on Brier (a squared-error metric where lower is better, scaled here on the 0-to-2 range the Goldstein paper uses):

ICPM: about 0.23. GJP's best aggregation method, an "All Surveys Logit" weighting that combined open-tournament forecasters' probabilistic estimates: about 0.15.

That's the puzzle. An open tournament's calibrated-aggregation procedure beat a U.S.-intelligence-community market on a one-year benchmark of shared unclassified questions.

The way this result usually gets told in popular accounts is something like "elite GJP teams beat the CIA by 25 to 30 percent". That phrasing collapses two findings that need to be kept apart. Goldstein's paper is a comparison of *aggregation methods*, with a clearly-stated caveat that the "best method" was selected ex post from a set of candidates the GJP team built. It isn't a claim that any individual GJP forecaster was sharper than any individual analyst, nor that the GJP team's training package caused the gap. We'll keep those questions separate; the second one is contested in section 6 in ways popular accounts haven't picked up.

What the result *does* say, even after those caveats, is that a calibrated probabilistic aggregation procedure run on open-tournament forecasters could outperform a single intelligence-community prediction market on a shared one-year benchmark of unclassified questions. The interesting question is then not "are amateurs better than experts" but "what does the system that won actually do, and which of its properties carry over to a single person who isn't running a tournament?" That's the rest of this post.

A historical note before the section closes. In 1876, late in his life, Charles Darwin wrote a short autobiography for his children. In it he described a single habit he claimed had shaped most of his work, a "golden rule" he had followed for many years. Section 4 quotes it in full. For now: 130 years before IARPA paid people to forecast, an English naturalist running an experiment on himself was already implementing a piece of what won.

### 3. The three asymmetries (and the contested fourth case)

The puzzle from section 2 has a long empirical pre-history. Three pieces of motivated-reasoning research from the 1970s, 1990s, and 2010s converge on the same picture: the brain handles evidence asymmetrically depending on whether the conclusion the evidence points to is one you'd already prefer. The book that put the case in front of a wide audience, [Michael Lewis's *The Undoing Project*](/blog/undoing-project) on Kahneman and Tversky's bias catalog, is the foundation Galef's frame stacks on. This section reaches behind that catalog to three of the specific empirical findings that make the catalog more than a metaphor, and then adds a fourth case the field is still arguing about.

**(a) Directional goals bias what your search retrieves.** Ziva Kunda's 1990 review article "[The Case for Motivated Reasoning](https://fbaum.unc.edu/teaching/articles/Psych-Bulletin-1990-Kunda.pdf)" is the canonical statement of the result. In Kunda's formulation, "directional goals affect reasoning by affecting which information will be considered in the reasoning process." The point is sharper than "smart people rationalize away inconvenient facts." It's that the brain doesn't *find* the inconvenient facts in the first place. Memory retrieval is biased; counterexamples that would surface if the directional goal were different stay below threshold. The reasoning that follows looks careful, careful inferences from a coherent body of evidence, and it is careful; it just operates on a curated input.

**(b) Identical evidence polarizes, not converges.** The 1979 [Lord, Ross, and Lepper study](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.37.11.2098) put 48 Stanford undergraduates with strong prior beliefs about capital punishment in front of a deliberately balanced packet of two studies, one supporting and one undermining the deterrence hypothesis. Both groups read the same studies. Both groups walked away more confident in their original positions than they had been before the experiment started. In the authors' words, "the result of exposing contending factions in a social dispute to an identical body of relevant empirical evidence may be not a narrowing of disagreement but rather an increase in polarization." Same evidence in. Greater disagreement out.

**(c) Preferred conclusions face lower evidentiary thresholds.** [Peter Ditto and David Lopez, in 1992](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.63.4.568), gave subjects a fake medical test for a fake "TAA enzyme" deficiency. Half the subjects were told the result they got indicated a serious health problem; the other half were told their result indicated they were fine. Subjects in the unfavorable-result condition spent longer scrutinizing the test, were more likely to retest, and were more likely to question the test's validity. In Ditto and Lopez's words: "information consistent with a preferred conclusion is examined less critically than information inconsistent with a preferred conclusion, and consequently, less information is required to reach the former than the latter." The bar moves with the conclusion.

These three are the spine of the motivated-reasoning literature, cited and elaborated in subsequent decades' work. Take them together and the personal-scale failure mode from section 1 looks less like a quirk and more like a default operating procedure: the brain retrieves selectively (Kunda), polarizes under identical evidence (Lord/Ross/Lepper), and applies a lower bar to conclusions you already prefer (Ditto/Lopez).

**(d) The contested fourth case: identity-protective cognition.** Dan Kahan's research program is the most recent addition to this picture. His [2017 paper "Motivated Numeracy and Enlightened Self-Government"](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2319992), with Peters, Dawson, and Slovic, took the Lord/Ross/Lepper-style polarization result and asked whether quantitative skill rescues you from it. The answer the paper reports: no, and worse than no. On a politically loaded contingency table about gun-control policy, more numerate subjects polarized along their existing political identity *more* than less numerate ones did, not less. Kahan's frame for this is *identity-protective cognition*: under the right conditions, the brain deploys whatever cognitive equipment it has, including numeracy, in service of conclusions consistent with the identity it's defending. The default-to-truth adaptation [Malcolm Gladwell describes in *Talking to Strangers*](/blog/talking-to-strangers), where the brain assumes the speaker isn't lying, is one of these sensible-but-narrow adaptations; default-to-defend-identity is another.

If that result held cleanly, it would be the most striking asymmetry of the four. It hasn't held cleanly. The replication-and-interpretation literature accumulated dissents in the years following the original finding. [Persson and colleagues' 2021 preregistered replication](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2021.104768) in *Cognition* "did not find good evidence for motivated numeracy" under preregistered criteria. [Connor and colleagues' replication in a Western European sample](https://doi.org/10.1017/bpp.2020.32) "replicate the basic motivated numeracy effect, but...do not find evidence of increased polarization among high-numeracy participants." That is, the small effect survived; the dramatic high-numeracy amplification didn't. [Glüer-Pagin and Spectre's 2024 conceptual critique](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13164-024-00737-w) argues the mechanism Kahan posits doesn't quite fit the data: the effect "involve[s] plenty of motivation to reason, but no motivated reasoning." These are not breaking-news dissents; they are the historical replication and conceptual context that accumulated through the early 2020s. Together they leave the Kahan case as a finding the post takes seriously without taking the strong dose-response form on faith.

So the post takes the broader idea, that quantitative skill can be deployed in service of identity and the architecture of cognition supports this kind of use, as the candidate mechanism. It explicitly hedges the specific high-numeracy-amplifies-polarization dose response. We're calling this the contested fourth case rather than the fourth asymmetry on purpose. It's a different kind of finding than (a) through (c): more recent, more disputed, more central to a particular political-psychology research program. The intuition payoff is sharper for being honest. Smart people fail at calibrated judgment because the architecture of cognition is built around three settled asymmetries, plus a fourth case whose specific dose response the literature has accumulated dissents about.

### 4. The architecture beneath: why the asymmetries are the default

The three settled asymmetries from section 3 don't behave like incidental glitches. The original studies report them as outputs of the way reasoning ordinarily works, not as failures of attention or training. Two research programs from the past two decades go further and offer a frame for *why* a normally-functioning brain would produce them.

**Reason is built for argument.** Hugo Mercier and Dan Sperber's [*The Enigma of Reason*](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674368309) (Harvard University Press, 2017) is the most-discussed recent statement of the case. Their argumentative theory holds that reasoning evolved primarily to produce and evaluate arguments in social-persuasive contexts, not to help the individual arrive at better beliefs in isolation. If that's the design brief, the asymmetries from section 3 stop looking like flaws. Confirmation bias starts looking like a working feature: it's how you build a strong case for your side. Polarization on identical evidence is what you'd expect from a system that's optimizing for "win the argument," not "converge on truth."

**Self-deception serves outward deception.** Robert Trivers's [*The Folly of Fools*](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9780465085972/thefollyoffools) (Basic Books, 2011) is the older complement. Trivers's central argument is that self-deception evolved as a mechanism that makes outward deception more credible. If the false belief is genuinely held, the deceiver transmits no involuntary tells. The brain has to be its own audience first; the listener is the second audience. The line that recurs in Trivers's interviews about the book is that the primary reason we fool ourselves is to fool others, and the reason we fool others is the same reason any social organism deceives: there's an information advantage on the line.

Mercier-Sperber and Trivers don't *prove* the §3 asymmetries are surface features of the architecture they describe; that's a step neither book attempts and no paper in the matrix establishes. What they offer is a frame in which directional retrieval, biased assimilation, and asymmetric thresholds stop looking like a personal failure and start looking like the kind of pattern you'd expect from a system shaped by the social uses of reasoning. The frame is conceptual, not causal. The §3 asymmetries are real on their own terms; what Act 2's diagnosis adds is a reason to expect them to be sticky.

This has a stronger consequence than "try harder to think well." It says a single reader, sitting alone, running self-tests on themselves, can only get so far against patterns that are likely social in origin. Section 7 will give you five of those self-tests anyway. The trainability literature in §9 will be honest about how strong a claim "self-tests like these reliably move outcomes" can carry; the short answer is "small but real for related interventions, and unstudied for Galef's specific tests in particular." Section 8 will insist that the architecture-shaped problem demands a *systems* layer too. The Mercier-Sperber and Trivers frames are what make that demand non-optional.

The historical point of contact is Darwin. In the autobiography he wrote in his last years, [published posthumously in 1887 by his son Francis](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2010/pg2010-images.html), Darwin gave one paragraph to a single working habit:

> "I had, also, during many years, followed a golden rule, namely, that whenever a published fact, a new observation or thought came across me, which was opposed to my general results, to make a memorandum of it without fail and at once; for I had found by experience that such facts and thoughts were far more apt to escape from memory than favourable ones. Owing to this habit, very few objections were raised against my views which I had not at least noticed and attempted to answer."

Read it twice. Darwin's contribution to the scout-mode toolkit isn't a thought experiment. It's an *artifact*. The notebook is an external system that catches the disconfirming observations the directional-goal-biased retrieval mechanism in Kunda's 1990 description would otherwise let drop. Run the architecture as designed, with no countermeasures, and Kunda's selection effect chews through the inconvenient evidence in days. Run the architecture *plus the notebook*, and the inconvenient evidence is on a timestamped line that's still legible six years later, when you're writing chapter twelve. Different game.

Section 8 returns to the artifact idea and generalizes it. The Darwin notebook is the personal-scale instance of a class of moves Galef calls operational habits and the GJP research program calls calibration tracking. Section 10 closes the post on the same notebook.

## Act 2 — The scout's measurable side

### 5. AOMT, Brier, and what calibration actually looks like

We left section 4 with a diagnosis (the §3 asymmetries are likely sticky for reasons the Mercier-Sperber and Trivers frames make plausible) and a single counter-move (Darwin's notebook, an external system that survives the brain doing the forgetting). Act 2 stops feeling the problem and starts measuring it. Two ideas, one section: the dispositional trait the GJP literature uses to talk about scouts, and the metric that lets "calibrated" be a number rather than a vibe.

**Actively open-minded thinking.** The trait has a name and an instrument. Jonathan Baron's textbook [*Thinking and Deciding*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/thinking-and-deciding/9F33EE43D2C3B3076EC4FBC9DF34F213) (4th edition, Cambridge University Press, 2008) defines actively open-minded thinking, AOMT, as the willingness to seek contrary evidence, to weigh new evidence against held beliefs, to tolerate complexity, and to update in proportion to evidence. The first published instrument that operationalizes the construct as a survey scale is [Stanovich and West's 1997 paper](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.89.2.342) in the *Journal of Educational Psychology*. The construct is a research operationalization of what Galef calls the scout orientation: not a temperamental trait you're born with, not "intellectual humility" as a virtue, but a measurable propensity to do specific things in the presence of disconfirming information.

In the Good Judgment Project, AOMT was *a* predictor of individual forecaster accuracy. The 2015 *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied* paper by [Mellers, Stone, Atanasov and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1037/xap0000040) lists it alongside several others, in the authors' own framing: "key predictors of accuracy were dispositional variables of cognitive ability, political knowledge, and open-mindedness; situational variables of training in probabilistic reasoning and participation in collaborative teams that shared information and discussed rationales; and behavioral variables of deliberation time and frequency of belief updating." That's a list of seven things. AOMT is one. It isn't "the strongest" predictor and it isn't single-handedly responsible for the GJP's results. The post will be careful to keep that framing because the literature does. What AOMT *is* is one trait the literature has measurably correlated with one outcome you'd plausibly want; whether the trait is cultivable, and how reliably, is the open question §9 returns to.

**Brier score.** The metric. For a binary forecast (the event happens or it doesn't), Brier score is the mean squared error between the probability you assigned and the outcome. If you said 0.7 and the event happened (outcome = 1), your error on that forecast is $(0.7 - 1)^2 = 0.09$. If you said 0.7 and it didn't happen (outcome = 0), your error is $(0.7)^2 = 0.49$. Average those squared errors over a long sequence of forecasts and you have your Brier score. Lower is better. Zero is perfect.

A convention note that matters because it bites later. [Glenn Brier's 1950 original paper](https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/mwre/78/1/1520-0493_1950_078_0001_vofeit_2_0_co_2.xml) defines the score on a 0-to-2 range for two-outcome forecasts. The Goldstein-vs-ICPM paper from section 2 reports its numbers on that same 0-to-2 scale; the ICPM ≈ 0.23 and GJP-best ≈ 0.15 sit on that range. Modern individual-forecaster Brier in the [Mellers et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614524255) and [2015](https://doi.org/10.1037/xap0000040) GJP papers is reported on the 0-to-1 scale you get from binary squared error. They're not different metrics; they're two scalings. The post says explicitly which scale is in play whenever a number appears. The 0.08 Brier gap from section 2 is on the 0-to-2 scale. That's a gap big enough to matter on a one-year benchmark and small enough that the right reading is "an aggregation procedure can do better than a market on questions like these," not "an aggregation procedure always wins."

A calibration plot is the picture behind the metric. Pool a forecaster's predictions by the probability they assigned (everything in the 0.7-bucket, everything in the 0.8-bucket), then ask what fraction of those events actually happened. A forecaster who said 70 percent and was right 70 percent of the time sits on the diagonal at the 0.7 mark. A forecaster who said 90 percent and was right 60 percent of the time is overconfident; on the plot, their curve sags below the diagonal at the high-probability end. Brier score is mean squared error over forecasts; the calibration plot shows one part of what determines the score (whether stated probabilities track observed frequencies), but two forecasters can have the same calibration curve and different Briers. The plot and the score are companions, not equivalents. The [Mellers 2015](https://doi.org/10.1037/xap0000040) *JEP: Applied* finding is that AOMT is *correlated* with higher individual forecasting accuracy in the GJP data, alongside several other predictors; whether that correlation reflects AOMT moving any specific component of someone's calibration curve is a separate question the matrix doesn't pin down. [Nassim Taleb's *Fooled by Randomness*](/blog/fooled-by-randomness) made the case fifteen years before the GJP did that the human mind isn't built for sophisticated probability; the calibration plot is the thing you measure when you decide to build the muscle anyway.

You can do this on yourself. The obvious caveat: a small number of forecasts gives a noisy curve, and most personal calibration tracking lives in the small-N regime. Section 8 returns to the personal-scale version of this practice in detail. The point in section 5 is that "I'm well-calibrated" stops being a claim about character once you can show the plot. It becomes a claim with a number attached, scored on a metric that's defined on a finite range, that someone else can reproduce from the same forecasts and outcomes.

### 6. The aggregator beats experts: Goldstein revisited, and the Hauenstein open question

Section 5 gave us the trait (AOMT) and the metric (Brier). With those in hand we can return to the puzzle from section 2 and ask the question we couldn't ask cleanly back then: what does the aggregation procedure that beat the ICPM actually do?

The honest answer is that the post can sketch the *kind* of thing it does qualitatively, and can't make a load-bearing primary-source claim about the exact arithmetic without pulling more weight from the GJP team's technical documentation than is reasonable. What follows is the post's own toy-model walkthrough, not a sourced claim from the matrix. The high-level shape: aggregating many probabilistic forecasts can reduce variance under specific assumptions about how the forecasters relate to each other, the two heaviest being (a) approximate independence between forecasters and (b) errors that aren't too strongly correlated. If both hold, ordinary statistics gives you something like a √n shrinking of the standard error. If correlation is tight, you're basically looking at one forecaster ten times, and the noise you wanted to wash out doesn't wash out. Treat that as the textbook explanation a numerate reader can sanity-check; it isn't claimed in the GJP papers.

Inset: <a href="https://doi.org/10.1177/09567976241266481">Hauenstein et al. 2025</a>'s IRT reanalysis of <a href="https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614524255">Mellers et al. 2014</a> reports the training-and-teaming effects shrank, vanished, or in some cases reversed once method-variance variables were controlled. The aggregation result (Goldstein) and the individual-causal-mechanism claim are different questions; the inset is the second one, kept separate from the funnel.</figcaption>
</figure>

Picture it as a funnel. One forecaster's distribution of estimates is wide and possibly off-center. That's panel (i). Ten independent forecasters with the same bias, averaged naively, give a narrower distribution centered on the same off-center mean. Variance shrinks; bias doesn't move. That's panel (ii). Ten *correlated* forecasters with the same bias, averaged the same way, give a distribution that's narrower than panel (i) but wider than panel (ii). Correlation eats the variance reduction. That's panel (iii). The funnel doesn't say "aggregation always beats truth." It says "aggregation can reduce variance under independence; that's the mechanism worth a name."

The GJP's "All Surveys Logit" is a more sophisticated procedure than a flat average. The post can stand behind two facts about it: it is the aggregator Goldstein and colleagues report as the "best-method" comparison against the ICPM (Brier .15 vs .23), and the paper itself flags that comparison as partly an ex-post selection across the GJP's aggregator family. The exact arithmetic, weighting schedule, and damping behavior are mechanical details the post does not put on a load-bearing line. The post is also not claiming All Surveys Logit reduces to the toy "ten independent forecasters" model from Fig 6; its real mechanics are richer than independent averaging. What the toy model is doing is showing the *direction* in which an aggregation procedure can produce gains; ASL produces a gain at scale, and Fig 6 shows you why an aggregation procedure can be the kind of thing that does.

The other side of the section-2 comparison, the ICPM, is a prediction market. Prediction markets have well-discussed failure modes in the broader literature. The post won't try to attribute the ICPM's 0.23 Brier to any specific one, because the Goldstein paper diagnoses the comparison without diagnosing the cause. We have an aggregation-method comparison and an outcome; we don't have a clean primary-source story about why the market lost. That's a finer-grained question the field hasn't published. The post leaves it open.

**Now the open question that matters most.** Section 2 made a careful split: Goldstein's algorithmic-aggregation comparison on one side, the [Mellers et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614524255) *Psychological Science* claims about training, teaming, and tracking on the other. The aggregation result holds. The training-and-teaming causal claim is the part that's contested.

[Hauenstein, Thomas, Illingworth, and Dougherty 2025](https://doi.org/10.1177/09567976241266481) in *Psychological Science* (online December 2024) reanalyzed Mellers's 2014 data using an item-response-theory framework that models forecasters' latent ability from their item-by-item choices. Under that reanalysis, with key extraneous variables controlled, the relationship between training/teaming and forecast accuracy "differed from the interpretation of the original findings." In plain-English terms: the effects shrank, in some specifications vanished, in some reversed sign. That isn't the same thing as "the GJP findings don't replicate." It's that the data are consistent with a smaller causal role for the specific interventions Mellers et al. credited, once method-variance variables are statistically controlled.

The post's framing is the obvious one: the aggregation result holds (Goldstein); the question of which causal mechanism drove individual-level forecasting gains is open (Hauenstein). One beat further: a literature that lets an IRT-reanalysis paper land in *Psychological Science* is itself running, at the field scale, a structural move the §3 asymmetries make hard inside any single skull. Post-publication critique at the literature level, which section 8 will return to, is what makes the field self-correcting without requiring any individual to be a saint.

Act 2 closes with two clean things and one open thing. AOMT is measurable. Brier is measurable. Aggregation under independence is a real mechanism with a math story behind it. The Mellers-Hauenstein dispute is the open piece. Act 3 takes both halves into section 7's individual moves and section 8's systems-layer moves.

## Act 3 — Personal scout mode (with the systems layer)

### 7. Galef's five self-tests (and what they're for)

Pick the belief from section 1. The one you'd defend with numbers if pressed. We're going to run thought experiments on it.

Galef's *The Scout Mindset* (2021) gives a working catalog of five thought experiments in Chapter 5, "Noticing Bias": the Double Standard Test, the Outsider Test, the Conformity Test, the Selective Skeptic Test, and the Status Quo Bias Test. The names are exact; the prompts below are paraphrased to fit the post's voice. Each is a specific question you can ask yourself about a specific belief.

- **Double Standard Test.** Would I judge this evidence the same way if it pointed to a different conclusion? If the same study, with the same effect size, supported the opposite political position, would I cite it?
- **Outsider Test.** If a stranger were in this situation, holding only the evidence I currently hold and not my history with the topic, what would I advise them?
- **Conformity Test.** If the people I respect, whose opinions I'd defer to in conversation, no longer held this view, would I still hold it on the strength of the evidence alone?
- **Selective Skeptic Test.** Would I scrutinize this piece of evidence with the same energy if it argued for the other side of the disagreement?
- **Status Quo Bias Test.** If I didn't already hold this position, if I were starting from scratch with the same evidence, would I adopt it now?

Each test is an attempt to give you a way to detach a single belief from the directional goal that's currently shaping how you process evidence about it. The tests aren't novel as a category. Decision-making research has used cousin thought experiments for decades; what Galef contributes is a small operational set you can actually run in five minutes on a Tuesday.

With that caveat, here's the reading. The Outsider Test is a Kunda counter-move: imagining a stranger in your position changes which information your brain decides to retrieve, partly washing out the directional-goal selection effect. The Selective Skeptic Test is a Ditto-Lopez counter-move: an explicit attempt to apply the same scrutiny budget to evidence that supports your conclusion as you do to evidence that doesn't. The Double Standard Test points at the same Ditto-Lopez asymmetry from the other direction. The Conformity Test pulls against the Mercier-Sperber social-justification mechanism: what if the audience your reasoning is building a case for didn't exist? The Status Quo Bias Test pulls against the Lord-Ross-Lepper polarization-by-prior mechanism by asking the brain to start the inference fresh. All of those are reasonable hypotheses about why the tests might work; none of them is established by a primary source the post can cite.

Why insist on the caveat at this length? Because you're about to take five tests into your daily decision-making, and the post owes you the honest level of confidence to attach to "this test fixes that bias." The honest level is narrower than common write-ups of Galef's framework imply: the tests are coherent prompts that map onto failure modes the empirical literature catalogues; the §9 trainability evidence is small-but-real for *related* debiasing interventions and silent on Galef's five specifically; the post's recommendation to run them on a belief is a "reasonable, cheap to try" call rather than a "the data says this works" call. Section 7 doesn't claim more than that.

### 8. The systems layer: scout mode at the institutional scale

Section 4 diagnosed soldier mode as social architecture. Section 7 gave you five thought experiments to run on yourself. The two don't fit together cleanly. If the architecture is social and the counter-program is individual, the counter-program doesn't quite address the diagnosis.

This section sketches the systems-layer moves that complete the diagnosis. None of them is a Galef invention. The post bundles them here because the *combination* with section 7 is what actually matches what section 4 said the problem is. Five thought experiments alone can only do so much. Five thought experiments plus precommitment plus calibration tracking plus aggregation plus literature-scale dispute-and-reanalysis covers more of the failure surface than the individual moves alone do; the post does not claim a specific organizational deployment of these moves is empirically validated, only that the structural moves match the section-4 diagnosis.

**Precommitment.** Before you see the new evidence, write down what evidence *would* change your mind on the belief from section 1. Be specific. Not "evidence to the contrary"; rather "if I learned that intervention X reduced outcome Y by less than Z percent in a registered RCT with N over 1,000, I would update toward the no-effect side." This sounds bureaucratic and is bureaucratic, on purpose. The bureaucracy is the point. The Kunda 1990 directional-goal selection effect from section 3 only has bite when you haven't named the disconfirming evidence in advance. Once you've named it, the brain has fewer degrees of freedom to filter it out as it arrives. The technique recurs in Galef's book and in a long history of decision-research practice; the post calls it precommitment because that's the canonical term.

**Calibration tracking.** Section 5 introduced Brier score and the calibration plot as the GJP's measurement discipline. The personal-scale version of that discipline is the same metric run on yourself. Pick a tracker, any tracker (a plain text file works), and write down your forecasts on questions whose outcome will be checked. Resolution dates close the loop. Score them later. With enough resolved forecasts to silence noise, you can plot your own calibration curve and read your own Brier score against the diagonal. [Mellers 2015](https://doi.org/10.1037/xap0000040) *JEP: Applied* lists "frequency of belief updating" as one of the seven predictors of GJP forecaster accuracy that correlated with higher accuracy in the tournament data. The personal-scale corollary is more modest than a causal claim: scoring yourself converts "I'm well-calibrated" from a self-image into a measurement that tells you what your priors are actually doing. The personal-scale practice is also one place where the open Hauenstein question matters less than the GJP-vs-ICPM result does. Scoring yourself doesn't depend on whether teaming caused the GJP team's gains. It depends on the metric being well-defined, which it is.

**Outside review and aggregation.** Section 6 said aggregation under independence is the mechanism worth a name. The same family of move generalizes downward, to small teams: independent probabilistic estimates, then averaging or logit-pooling. The strength of any gain depends on the same conditions the funnel needed (independence between estimators, comparable calibration, errors that aren't tightly correlated). Those conditions often fail inside a team that talks before forecasting; the conversation correlates the errors. Decision-architecture techniques like blind first-pass forecasts, dissenting-view-captured-in-writing, and red-team review are attempts to preserve some of the independence the funnel from section 6 needed. The post will not claim a specific decision-architecture move is empirically validated for general decision-making; the literature is much smaller and weaker than the GJP's. The structural move is the one with the math story behind it: aggregation works under independence; preserve some of the independence, get some of the variance reduction.

**Post-publication critique and adversarial review.** The systems-layer analog of Galef's individual self-tests. The strictest version is adversarial collaboration: a person with a strong prior and a person with the opposite strong prior agree, in advance, on a research design or a piece of evidence whose outcome they both predict differently, write down their predictions, run the test, and the loser updates publicly. The looser, more common version is post-publication critique: a published reanalysis by a research team that did not pre-commit with the original authors but is willing to put their counter-claim in print. The Hauenstein-vs-Mellers situation from section 6 is the looser version, not adversarial collaboration; it is still a literature-scale instance of the same family of move, because the dispute is now legible in the same shared record. Smaller versions of either pattern can run inside a team or across two teams that disagree. The trick is the explicit prediction in step one; without it, both sides can claim retrospectively that the outcome confirmed their prior.

### 9. The trainability picture: small effects, unresolved transfer (and what this means for the closing move)

The sober counterpart to sections 7 and 8. Can you actually train scout mode? The literature says: yes, somewhat, with caveats. The caveats are the load-bearing input to section 10's closing prescription.

**Single-session debiasing training.** [Morewedge and colleagues 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/2372732215600886), in *Policy Insights from the Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, ran a study with two intervention modalities and six target biases (anchoring, bias blind spot, confirmation bias, fundamental attribution error, projection bias, representativeness). The interactive-game intervention with personalized feedback and practice reduced these biases by at least 31.94 percent immediately and by at least 23.57 percent at two-month follow-up. The video intervention (a more passive, lower-investment format) reduced them by at least 18.60 percent immediately and 19.20 percent at two-month follow-up. The headline number you'll see in popular accounts is "30 percent reduction"; the per-modality numbers are what the paper actually reports, and the gap between them matters. The game beats the video, on the same biases, with the same outcome measure.

**Field transfer.** A 2019 *Psychological Science* paper by [Sellier, Scopelliti, and Morewedge](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797619861429) tested whether the bench effects translated to a Shuttle-Challenger-modeled business case where trained participants made an actual decision under hypothesis-confirmation pressure. The paper's headline number was 29 percent. A [2020 corrigendum](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797620930211) corrects that figure to 19 percent: a calculation error in the original. The qualitative conclusion (training helps in a field-transfer task) holds; the size shrinks. Anyone repeating the 29 percent number in 2026 is reading the original paper without the correction; the post uses 19 percent because that's the corrected figure.

**The systematic-review picture.** A 2025 *Nature Human Behaviour* systematic review and meta-analysis by [Swaryandini, Graham, Griffith, Grilo and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-025-02253-y) covers educational debiasing interventions across 54 randomized controlled trials and 10,941 participants. The meta-analytic estimate, computed across 160 effect sizes from a 41-study subset, is a Hedges *g* of 0.26 (95% confidence interval 0.14 to 0.39). The authors are explicit about the limitation that follows: "questions remain about the depth and transferability of learning beyond classroom settings...more research is needed to determine whether these improvements translate to meaningful changes in real-world decision-making." About a quarter of a standard deviation is not nothing; it's also not the kind of number you'd reorganize your life around.

A directly analyst-relevant 2025 study by [Heerma van Voss and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-28794-w) in *Scientific Reports* runs the experiment on a population that better matches the post's scenario: 71 national-level risk analysts (recruited at the closing conference of a National Risk Assessment in a European country) and 118 master's students in risk-related programs. The intervention is short, about 40 minutes, a scripted explanation of decision biases plus the Carter Racing case-study group exercise. The design is pretest/posttest within-subject. The main effect of the intervention on confirmation bias was an *F*(1, 169) of 20.7, *p* < 0.001, partial *η²* of 0.109. Analysts and students benefited equally. That doesn't resolve the long-run-transfer question Swaryandini's authors flagged. It does answer a narrower one: a 40-minute structured-workshop session reduced confirmation bias on the post-test in a group of working national-level risk analysts as well as in students, on the post-test administered in the same workshop.

The four findings together make a coherent picture. Single-session debiasing training, in well-designed studies with feedback and practice, shifts measured bias on the same task by 20 to 30 percent. Field-transfer studies retain the qualitative effect but with smaller magnitudes (Sellier 19 percent). The meta-analytic estimate across the larger educational-intervention literature is a Hedges *g* of 0.26: not enormous, not negligible. And the limitation that runs through all of this is the one Swaryandini's authors name explicitly: real-world decision-making transfer is unresolved. The literature has measured short-term effects and a few field-transfer effects; what it hasn't measured cleanly is whether the trained habits stick six months later in the wild and on different decisions than the ones the training was about.

This shapes section 10. If trainability evidence supported a transformation, the closing move could be a twelve-step program. It doesn't, so the closing move can't be. The post's prescription has to be the smallest, most durable habit that survives the literature's actual effect sizes. Picking a habit that promises more than the literature's effect sizes can deliver would be a soldier-mode move; the prescription needs to come in under what the evidence supports, not above. Section 10 prescribes a single notebook habit, *because* the trainability evidence doesn't support a more ambitious one.

### 10. Darwin's golden rule, operationalized for 2026

Pull the whole post into one move. Write the disconfirming thing down the moment it shows up.

Hedge first, before the prescription. The post is not saying every primary source it cites endorses this exact habit. None of them does, on its own. Darwin documented the practice on himself, in 1876, and reported its effect on his work. The GJP individual-level findings list "frequency of belief updating" as one of seven predictors of forecast accuracy. Galef's five thought experiments are cognitive prompts that ask you to notice contrary evidence in the moment, but they don't prescribe "write it down in a notebook." What the move does is recur across the post's primary sources as the smallest counter-move that's consistent with what each of them is doing. The trainability evidence in section 9 caps how strong a prescription the post can defend; the trainability evidence supports "small but real". A single-habit prescription that comes in under what the literature has measured is the honest closing move. Anything more ambitious would be the post lying about its own evidence.

The protocol fits on a small card.

1. When an observation, fact, or argument arrives that's opposed to a belief you currently hold, write it down. The same day. Plain text file or paper notebook; both work. Tooling doesn't matter; latency does.
2. Date it. Name the belief it threatens. One sentence is enough.
3. Re-read what you've written every few months. The Kunda 1990 retrieval-bias mechanism would otherwise erase it; the timestamped line on the page resists that.

That's it. Section 8's systems-layer moves (precommitment registers, calibration logs, post-publication critique) are larger, more specialized cousins of this single individual move; the personal notebook is the smallest version that survives the brain doing the forgetting. If you later want a calibration log, that's a different artifact built from a different input (timestamped probabilistic forecasts plus their resolutions). If you want a precommitment register, that's another. The notebook is not all of those at once. It's the smallest one, and the one most likely to actually get used.

The diagram in Figure 10 puts the move at three scales. Darwin's notebook is the innermost scale. The GJP forecaster's calibration log is the middle scale. The Mellers-Hauenstein dispute, with the 2014 paper and the 2025 reanalysis sitting on the same record, is the outermost scale. The shapes share a four-step backbone: an observation arrives, the holder of the prior asks whether it's opposed to the current model, the observation gets recorded, it gets re-encountered at the next review. The move at the literature scale takes ten years; the move at the personal scale takes a minute. The shapes rhyme more than they match. Inner-ring Darwin is the cleanest instance; the middle and outer rings are structural analogs that share the four-step skeleton but do considerably more than that skeleton describes.

Section 6 left the Hauenstein-vs-Mellers dispute as an open question with no published response. That's the move at the literature scale, in flight, in 2026. A 2014 paper made a causal claim. A 2025 reanalysis tested it. The next step is the response paper, or the absence of one, and the field's decision about what to do with the absence. Whatever happens, the dispute is on the record. The same way a dated line in your notebook will be on the record when you sit down to make a decision in 2027.

Darwin's notebook from 1876, the GJP's "All Surveys Logit" from 2015, and the Hauenstein reanalysis from 2025 are not the same procedure. They aren't even close to the same procedure. The aggregator combines forecasts; the reanalysis re-tests a published causal claim; the notebook records a disconfirming observation. What they share is more modest than a single backbone: each makes the failure mode of the system less likely to be silently absorbed. The notebook resists Kunda-style retrieval bias. The aggregator resists the variance of any one forecaster. The reanalysis resists the original interpretation surviving by inertia.
