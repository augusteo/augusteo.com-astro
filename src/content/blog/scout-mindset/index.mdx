*A mechanism-first take on Julia Galef's 2021 book* The Scout Mindset. *Not a book review. The post walks the empirical literature on motivated reasoning, identity-protective cognition, and the Good Judgment Project, then prescribes the smallest durable habit consistent with what the sources actually say. About a 30-minute read.*

### 1. A Tuesday morning, and the belief I keep losing arguments about

Picture the demo I've shown a hundred times. A PDF viewer, a commercial floor plan, an AI overlay drawing red rectangles over walls. An estimator sitting next to me, clicking through the count, comparing the AI's tally to her manual takeoff from last week. Six walls flagged wrong: two missed, four false positives on an HVAC shaft wall that isn't structural. She looks at me. I look at the screen.

That scene is a demo construct. I have run it enough times that I know where the friction lands, which product decisions made it worse, which made it better. I am also the person selling the software. That is the conflict I want to name before naming the belief, because the belief is directionally convenient for me: AI-assisted quantity takeoff cuts estimator hours by roughly 40% on commercial bid packages, and that gain holds across estimators rather than just the early adopters. I have said a version of that sentence in sales calls, in investor updates, in Slack messages to my own team. I believe it. I also have a financial stake in believing it.

Take that belief. Now imagine the same piece of evidence reaching your inbox twice.

First version: a contingency table, no labels attached. Two groups of estimators. Group A used the new workflow; Group B used the old one. The table shows hours logged and packages completed. You run the arithmetic. The productivity difference is 38%. You note it, update a little, file it under "promising but small sample."

Second version: the same table, same numbers, same arithmetic. But now the header reads "Boon AI-Assisted Takeoff: Vendor Efficacy Study." And you know I built Boon. The 38% is still 38%. Your update is not the same.

{/* FIGURE: §1 — two-updates sketch with 40% takeoff belief as loaded framing; rendered in task 9 */}

The numbers are identical. The conclusion the arithmetic points to is identical. What changed is whether the labels attach the evidence to something you already have a position on, something where agreeing or disagreeing costs you something socially or professionally. The brain applies more scrutiny to inconvenient evidence and less to convenient evidence, and it does this without announcing itself. You don't feel like you're doing motivated reasoning. You feel like you're being careful.

This is the small case the rest of the post hangs on. Not a political controversy, not a hot-button policy debate. A vendor productivity claim, with an author who has a known stake. Small enough to hold in your head, live enough that the asymmetry actually bites.

One person's read on this is a noisy estimate. The interesting question is what a less-noisy procedure even looks like, and that question gets its own section: §4.

### 2. Why I am not a neutral observer

I am the founder of Boon. Boon sells AI-assisted quantity takeoff to commercial estimating teams. My compensation moves with whether that product works at the scale I've been claiming it works. That is a conflict of interest in the plain sense, and it doesn't disappear because I'm aware of it.

But the conflict runs deeper than a direct financial stake. It also lives in the information pipeline.

When an estimator on one of our accounts tries the new workflow and it goes well, someone in sales knows by the end of that week. The customer success note lands in a Slack channel I'm in. Sometimes I get a screenshot. The signal travels fast. When a pilot fails, or when an estimator quietly reverts to the old workflow two weeks after training, the signal travels differently. It may surface in a quarterly support review. It may surface as a churn risk flag four months later. In several cases I found out about a failed pilot when I asked about it directly, not because it escalated to me. The information pipeline between what happens in the field and what reaches my attention is not symmetric. It is filtered by who has an incentive to tell me.

An estimator who goes through our onboarding and finds it doesn't fit her workflow knows that immediately. Her manager may notice the reversion two weeks later. It reaches me, if it reaches me, weeks or months after that, attenuated by layers of interpretation. The demo wins land the same day. The failed pilots land two layers downstream, often without a label that identifies them as failed pilots.

{/* FIGURE: §2 — evidence pipeline: world events filtered by demo wins, support escalations, sales calls, failed pilots two layers away, investor narrative; rendered in task 10 */}

The public-writing problem is the same asymmetry, running in a different channel. When I write a Twitter thread on AI takeoff productivity, my implicit model of the audience is a potential investor, an enterprise decision-maker, or someone considering whether to work at Boon. When I write an investor update, the audience is investors. When I give a conference talk, the audience is prospective customers and peers who will form opinions about the category. None of those audiences are this reader, and none of those writing acts are calibrated to help this reader update accurately. They are calibrated to persuade. I do not think of myself as being dishonest in those contexts; I think of myself as doing communications. That is the problem. The calibration happens below the threshold where I'd notice it as a bias.

So: I have a financial stake in the thing I'm trying to assess. I have an information pipeline that over-delivers evidence in favor of it. I have public-writing habits calibrated to convince audiences who share my directional interest. Three separate mechanisms, all pulling the same direction.

Section 6 will say what I'm going to do about it. The rest of this post is what I learned trying to figure out what to do.

### 3. What's actually happening inside the head

Naming the conflict from §2 is not enough. Knowing that I hold a belief I keep losing arguments about doesn't explain why the bias persists after I've identified it. The architecture of cognition makes it survive exactly that kind of awareness. What follows is a walk through what the brain actually does with takeoff-claim evidence: three settled asymmetries, a contested fourth case, and then the deeper frame that explains why knowing all this still leaves the problem mostly unsolved.

**Directional retrieval.** The first asymmetry is upstream of reasoning altogether. [Kunda's canonical statement of the result](https://fbaum.unc.edu/teaching/articles/Psych-Bulletin-1990-Kunda.pdf) locates the effect at the retrieval stage: "Directional goals affect reasoning by affecting which information will be considered in the reasoning process." The brain doesn't find the inconvenient facts and then explain them away. It doesn't retrieve them in the first place. Counterexamples that would surface if the directional goal were different stay below threshold, and the reasoning that follows looks careful, and is careful, but it operates on a curated input. Apply this to the takeoff belief. When I'm thinking about my 40% claim, which case studies does my brain surface? Which estimator-hours papers? Which industry survey numbers? The evidence pool I'm reasoning from is not neutral. The directional goal has already run a filter before I've written down the first consideration. The careful inference that follows is careful reasoning on a biased sample.

**Biased assimilation.** The second asymmetry operates on evidence after it arrives. Imagine two estimators read the same balanced case study: one AI takeoff pilot that won, one that lost, written up with comparable detail and methodological care. The estimator who already trusts the 40% claim walks away more confident in it. The estimator who already doubts it walks away more confident in the opposite. Same packet in. Greater disagreement out. That's the pattern the [1979 Lord, Ross, and Lepper study](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.37.11.2098) documented: 48 Stanford undergraduates with strong prior beliefs about capital punishment read a deliberately balanced set of two studies, and both groups ended up more confident in their original positions than when they started. The mechanism is not that people ignore the counter-evidence. They engage with it, and engage with it in ways that reinforce the position they came in with. For the takeoff belief, this means a balanced case-study portfolio is not a path to convergence. It is a path to the believers and the skeptics each feeling more vindicated.

**Asymmetric thresholds.** The third asymmetry is about how much evidence is enough. The bar for accepting a conclusion you'd prefer is lower than the bar for accepting one you wouldn't. [Ditto and Lopez's 1992 experiment](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.63.4.568) gave subjects a fake test result for a fake enzyme deficiency. Subjects who got bad news retested, scrutinized the test longer, and questioned its validity more. Subjects who got good news filed it quickly. In Ditto and Lopez's formulation: "Less information is required to reach the former than the latter." Translate to the takeoff belief: a study showing that AI takeoff fails on a particular package type gets re-read, gets methodological questions, gets "but were the estimators well-trained?" A study showing that AI takeoff succeeds at a comparable scale gets a mental "told you so" and moves to the confirmation pile. The bar isn't fixed. It moves with the conclusion, and it moves automatically, without any conscious choice to apply extra scrutiny to the bad news.

**The contested fourth case.** Dan Kahan's research program asks a harder question: does quantitative skill rescue you from the three asymmetries above? His [2017 paper with Peters, Dawson, and Slovic](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2319992) tested numeracy against identity-protective cognition on a politically loaded contingency table. The result the paper reports: more numerate subjects polarized along identity lines *more* than less numerate ones, not less. The frame is *identity-protective cognition*: the brain deploys whatever cognitive equipment it has, including statistical literacy, in service of conclusions consistent with the identity it's defending. Apply this to the takeoff belief: a numerate estimator who is skeptical of AI takeoff doesn't get less polarized when she's better at reading effect-size tables. She may get more polarized, because her statistical literacy lets her construct sharper objections. Being good at the tools doesn't immunize you against deploying them directionally.

That result, if it held cleanly, would be the most striking of the four. It hasn't held cleanly. Two preregistered replications found the basic effect (numeracy doesn't rescue you) but not the dramatic high-numeracy amplification. The dose response, the specific claim that more skill means more polarization, did not survive under preregistered criteria. The dissents from those replications and from a subsequent conceptual critique are detailed in the figure caption below. The take-home from the Kahan literature is the basic finding: quantitative skill is not a defense. It is not the stronger claim that skill actively amplifies the problem.

{/* FIGURE: §3 — biased assimilation as four operational panels: retrieve / weigh / threshold / identity alarm; rendered in task 11 */}

**The architecture that produces these patterns is social.** Mercier and Sperber's argumentative theory of reasoning holds that reasoning evolved primarily to produce and evaluate arguments in social-persuasive contexts, not to help the individual arrive at better private beliefs. If that's the design brief, the three asymmetries above stop looking like flaws. Directional retrieval is what you'd expect from a system optimized to build a case for your side. Biased assimilation is the natural output of a system that's running "win the argument" rather than "converge on truth." Trivers's complementary frame closes the loop on the self-deception side: self-deception evolved as a mechanism that makes outward deception more credible. If the false belief is genuinely held, the deceiver leaks no involuntary tells. The brain has to be its own audience first. Put Mercier and Sperber together with Trivers and the asymmetries stop looking like bugs in an otherwise truth-seeking machine. They look like surface features of a system shaped by a very different design brief. The architecture that produces these patterns is social, which is why the rest of this post can't end with five thought experiments.

**Darwin's notebook.** In the autobiography he wrote in his last years, [published posthumously in 1887 by his son Francis](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2010/pg2010-images.html), Darwin gave one paragraph to a single working habit:

> "I had, also, during many years, followed a golden rule, namely, that whenever a published fact, a new observation or thought came across me, which was opposed to my general results, to make a memorandum of it without fail and at once; for I had found by experience that such facts and thoughts were far more apt to escape from memory than favourable ones. Owing to this habit, very few objections were raised against my views which I had not at least noticed and attempted to answer."

Darwin's contribution here is not a thought experiment or a self-test. It is an *artifact*. The notebook is an external system that catches the disconfirming observations that directional retrieval would otherwise let drop. Run the architecture as designed, with no countermeasures, and the inconvenient evidence disappears in days. Run the architecture plus the notebook, and the inconvenient evidence is on a timestamped line that is still legible six years later, when you are writing chapter twelve. The artifact framing governs every subsequent move in this post: §4's calibration log, §6's precommitment register, §7's takeoff log are all instances of the same class. The notebook is where §3 ends and §4 begins.

### 4. How would I know if my belief is getting more accurate?

§3 ended on Darwin's notebook as the artifact-class counter-move: not a thought experiment, but an external system that catches what directional retrieval would otherwise drop. The natural follow-up is the question §4 takes seriously: if a single mind is biased in the ways §3 documented, what is a less-noisy procedure for assessing whether the 40% takeoff claim is actually supported? The rest of this section walks that procedure.

**Brier score: a metric for "was I right about the 40% claim."** For a binary forecast (the event happens or it doesn't), Brier score is the mean squared error between the probability you assigned and the outcome. Worked example: I forecast 0.7 that this estimator saves 40% on the next package and they save 32%, so the "saved 40%" event didn't happen. My error on that forecast is $(0.7 - 0)^2 = 0.49$. If they had saved 40%, the event would have happened and my error would be $(0.7 - 1)^2 = 0.09$. Average those squared errors over many forecasts and you have your Brier score. Lower is better. Zero is perfect. The metric matters because "I'm well-calibrated about AI takeoff" stops being a claim about character once you can score it. It becomes a number, defined on a finite range, reproducible from the same forecasts and outcomes by anyone who cares to check.

**The calibration plot.** Pool forecasts by the probability you assigned (everything in the 0.7-bucket, everything in the 0.8-bucket), then ask what fraction of those events actually happened. A forecaster who said 70 percent and was right 70 percent of the time sits on the diagonal at the 0.7 mark. A forecaster who said 90 percent and was right 60 percent of the time is overconfident; their curve sags below the diagonal at the high-probability end. The plot is the picture; the Brier score is its summary number. Two forecasters can have the same calibration curve and different Brier scores, because the score also captures resolution (how far your stated probabilities are from 0.5), not just calibration. The figure below shows both patterns on one set of axes.

**AOMT, one predictor among seven.** The trait has a name and a survey-instrument operationalization: actively open-minded thinking (AOMT), defined by Jonathan Baron and operationalized in a scale by Stanovich and West as the propensity to seek contrary evidence, weigh it against held beliefs, and update in proportion to it. In the Good Judgment Project (GJP), run by Tetlock and Mellers, AOMT was a predictor of individual forecaster accuracy. It was one of seven the GJP team identified: cognitive ability, political knowledge, open-mindedness, training in probabilistic reasoning, team participation, deliberation time, and frequency of belief updating. It is not the strongest predictor; it is not single-handedly responsible for the GJP results. What AOMT is: the disposition you'd want if you're trying to honestly assess the takeoff claim. It doesn't tell you the claim is right; it tells you your approach to evidence is at least disposed toward updating in proportion to it.

**The GJP-vs-ICPM result.** An IARPA tournament let calibrated aggregation procedures and a CIA-run prediction market (ICPM) answer the same questions for a year. The aggregation procedure won, on Brier, by a clear margin. In [the technical comparison paper](https://goodjudgment.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/Goldstein-et-al-GJP-vs-ICPM.pdf) by Goldstein and colleagues, the ICPM scored about 0.23 and the GJP's best aggregation method scored about 0.15, both on the 0-to-2 scale used in that paper. The paper itself flags that the "best method" comparison was partly an ex-post selection from a candidate aggregator family the GJP team built; that caveat belongs here, in one sentence. The point is the proof of concept: a calibrated aggregation procedure can beat a market on shared questions. That's a procedural result, not a personality-trait result.

**The aggregation funnel, walked with ten estimators.** Why does aggregating help? Imagine ten independent estimators each forecast the takeoff savings on the next package, no conversation, no shared spreadsheet. Their estimates form a distribution: some too high, some too low, some near the truth. Average them. Variance shrinks by roughly a factor of the square root of ten. The bias of the average is the same as the bias of any one estimator, but the noise around it is smaller. Now imagine the ten talked first and arrived at a group estimate. Their estimates correlate. The variance reduction is partial. Correlation eats the gain. The funnel is intuitive: independence is what gives aggregation its bite. The operational move "blind first-pass, then discuss" is the structural way to preserve some of that independence. If the ten estimators all read the same account of the last AI takeoff pilot before submitting their number, their errors are correlated, and the funnel closes partway. If they submit blind and discuss afterward, at least some of the independence survives.

### 5. Galef's five tests, run on the takeoff belief

Section 4 gave you a procedural answer: use Brier scores, calibration curves, and aggregation to tell whether a belief is tracking evidence over time. That's the measurement layer. Section 5 is the diagnostic layer: five thought experiments from Galef's Chapter 5 that you can run on a single belief in five minutes on a Tuesday. Pick the belief from §1. We're going to put it through five passes.

**Double Standard Test.** Would I judge a competitor's 40% claim with the same scrutiny I'd apply to Boon's? What study design would I demand from a competitor's pilot that I haven't demanded from my own? Running this on the takeoff belief surfaces something specific: I'm applying a generous evidentiary standard to data my own team collected and a stringent one to everyone else's. That's not a rare thinking error. It's a patterned one, and the test's value is that it makes the gap visible rather than leaving it implicit in which objections I raise and which I don't.

**Outsider Test.** If a stranger were in this position, holding only the evidence I currently hold and not my years of demos and customer conversations, what would I advise them about the 40% claim? The test surfaces something uncomfortable: most of what makes my conviction feel reasonable is path dependence, not evidence. The stranger hasn't seen the demos I've run a hundred times. She sees the data, and the data alone is thinner than my conviction implies. That gap between "what the data supports" and "what my conviction implies" is exactly what the test is designed to expose.

**Conformity Test.** If my YC batch and my construction-tech peer group moved off the 40% claim, would I still hold it on the strength of the evidence alone? Running this reveals that a chunk of my conviction is downstream of who's in the room with me. That's not automatically wrong; peer expertise carries real information. But peer consensus shouldn't be doing the evidentiary work I'm currently letting it do, and the test forces me to separate those two loads.

**Selective Skeptic Test.** Do I scrutinize a pilot that under-delivered as harshly as I celebrate one that over-delivered? Running this on the takeoff belief surfaces a longer list of methodological objections to under-performing pilots than to over-performing ones. I find methodological reasons to discount a bad result that I don't find when the result is good. That gap is the asymmetric threshold from §3, running on me in real time, not as an abstract cognitive pattern but as a specific accounting of which objections I generated and which I didn't.

**Status Quo Bias Test.** If I were starting from scratch in 2026 with the evidence I have now, and not the path-dependent commitment to selling this software, would I still adopt the 40% claim with the confidence I currently hold it? My position on this belief has been compounding for several years. Some of that compounding is genuine learning from customers and deployments. Some of it is sunk cost. The test doesn't decompose those two for you, but it puts the question on the table in a way that staying inside your normal belief-update rhythm doesn't.

{/* FIGURE: §5 — Galef worksheet matrix + pressure map; rendered in task 13 */}

The tests are fast and individual. You can run all five on a single belief before your next meeting. What they can't reach, on their own, is the team-scale architecture where the same motivated-reasoning mechanisms operate across people rather than inside one person's head. Section 6 is the complement: what to do when the individual tests aren't enough because the belief is held collectively and the asymmetries are load-bearing for the whole organization.

### 6. Make it social: precommitment, outside review, blinded estimator trials

§3 located soldier mode in social architecture: the incentives that reward directional conclusions, the team cultures that treat confident predictions as identity signals, the vendor relationships that create financial stakes in specific forecasts. §5 ran five thought experiments inside one head. That's useful, and it's partial. Most of the failure surface §3 described lives outside any single head. This section is the complement: precommitment, outside review, blinded aggregation, and post-publication critique as structural moves applied to the takeoff belief specifically.

**Precommitment.** The Kunda 1990 directional-goal selection effect from §3 works by making the disconfirming evidence invisible at the moment it arrives. The fix is to name the disconfirming evidence before it arrives. This sounds bureaucratic, and it is. The bureaucracy is the point. Once the criteria are written down, the brain has fewer degrees of freedom to reframe an inconvenient result as "not quite what I had in mind."

Here is what a precommitment artifact looks like for the 40% takeoff-hours claim. This is an *illustrative* example of the kind of thing such a document would say, not a binding commitment by this post's author. The point of showing it in detail is to make visible what these entries actually carry: specific, testable criteria tied to resolution conditions that were set before the evidence was seen.

> *Precommitment register entry, 2026-05-08*
>
> *Belief held: AI-assisted takeoff reduces estimator hours by approximately 40% on commercial construction packages in the $2M–$20M range, relative to manual takeoff.*
>
> *Criteria that would update this belief downward:*
>
> *If, across three blinded pilots on packages over $2M with non-volunteer estimators, the median estimator-hours saved comes in below 25%, that is evidence the gain doesn't generalize beyond early adopters.*
>
> *If re-takeoff time after revisions for second-year platform users is not statistically distinguishable from first-month users on the same package class, that is evidence the productivity gain is a learning-curve artifact rather than a tool-driven gain.*
>
> *If a competitor's tool, run by their estimator on a Boon-pilot package class, achieves comparable hours-saved within ±10%, that is evidence the gain is real but not differentiating to the product.*
>
> *Resolution date: when the three pilots referenced above complete.*

The specific percentages and package sizes in that example are illustrative. What the format does is make the disconfirmation legible before the outcome is known, which is the structural move. Any precommitment that says only "I'll update if the evidence is against me" has done nothing; the work is in naming which evidence, at which magnitude, with which resolution conditions.

**Outside review.** A competitor's estimator, or an independent estimating consultancy, runs the same package blind. The structural point is that "show the customer the AI overlay and ask if they're impressed" is a vendor demo. That's a different test from "give the same input to a reviewer who has no stake in the result, and record what their estimate is." The second test doesn't require the reviewer to be impressed, or to agree about the hours saved, or to say anything positive at all. It requires them to produce an estimate. The comparison of that estimate to the AI-assisted one is the data. The independence of the reviewer is what makes the comparison meaningful.

**Blinded aggregation.** §4 described how the GJP's funnel structure converted individual probabilistic estimates into an aggregated forecast that outperformed the intelligence-community's analysts. The same structural move works at smaller scales. Ten independent estimators do a first-pass on the same package, blind, before any discussion. Conversation correlates errors; the blind first-pass is what preserves the independence the funnel from §4 needed. Once estimators know what each other think, the estimates are no longer independent draws, and the aggregation loses much of its variance-reduction benefit. The takeoff-belief application: ten estimators, same package, same input documentation, submit their hours-saved estimate before any discussion session, then discuss. The mean or logit-pooled aggregate of the blind estimates is a better estimator of the true figure than any post-discussion consensus, for the same reason §4's funnel worked.

**Post-publication critique as the literature-scale instance.** A 2014 paper made causal claims about training and teaming as the mechanism behind GJP forecaster gains. [The IRT reanalysis](https://doi.org/10.1177/09567976241266481) published in 2025 re-tested those claims under stricter methodological controls. The effects shrank; in some specifications they vanished; in some they reversed sign. The aggregation result (aggregated forecasts beat individual ones) holds. The causal mechanism claim (training and teaming caused the gains) is still being argued in print. That's what the precommitment-plus-publish-the-result move looks like at field scale: a published claim, a published reanalysis, an open shared record where the dispute is legible to anyone who reads both papers. The looser institutional version, short of pre-agreed adversarial collaboration, is still the same family of move because the counter-claim is on record rather than held in private disagreement.

§6 is the architecture layer. The moves here are slower to start than the five tests in §5, and they require cooperation from people beyond you. §7 is the smallest version of all of this that survives at the personal scale: one habit, one notebook, one dated line.

### 9. The trainability picture: small effects, unresolved transfer (and what this means for the closing move)

The sober counterpart to sections 7 and 8. Can you actually train scout mode? The literature says: yes, somewhat, with caveats. The caveats are the load-bearing input to section 10's closing prescription.

**Single-session debiasing training.** [Morewedge and colleagues 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/2372732215600886), in *Policy Insights from the Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, ran a study with two intervention modalities and six target biases (anchoring, bias blind spot, confirmation bias, fundamental attribution error, projection bias, representativeness). The interactive-game intervention with personalized feedback and practice reduced these biases by at least 31.94 percent immediately and by at least 23.57 percent at two-month follow-up. The video intervention (a more passive, lower-investment format) reduced them by at least 18.60 percent immediately and 19.20 percent at two-month follow-up. The headline number you'll see in popular accounts is "30 percent reduction"; the per-modality numbers are what the paper actually reports, and the gap between them matters. The game beats the video, on the same biases, with the same outcome measure.

**Field transfer.** A 2019 *Psychological Science* paper by [Sellier, Scopelliti, and Morewedge](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797619861429) tested whether the bench effects translated to a Shuttle-Challenger-modeled business case where trained participants made an actual decision under hypothesis-confirmation pressure. The paper's headline number was 29 percent. A [2020 corrigendum](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797620930211) corrects that figure to 19 percent: a calculation error in the original. The qualitative conclusion (training helps in a field-transfer task) holds; the size shrinks. Anyone repeating the 29 percent number in 2026 is reading the original paper without the correction; the post uses 19 percent because that's the corrected figure.

**The systematic-review picture.** A 2025 *Nature Human Behaviour* systematic review and meta-analysis by [Swaryandini, Graham, Griffith, Grilo and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-025-02253-y) covers educational debiasing interventions across 54 randomized controlled trials and 10,941 participants. The meta-analytic estimate, computed across 160 effect sizes from a 41-study subset, is a Hedges *g* of 0.26 (95% confidence interval 0.14 to 0.39). The authors are explicit about the limitation that follows: "Questions remain about the depth and transferability of learning beyond classroom settings...more research is needed to determine whether these improvements translate to meaningful changes in real-world decision-making." About a quarter of a standard deviation is not nothing; it's also not the kind of number you'd reorganize your life around.

A directly analyst-relevant 2025 study by [Heerma van Voss and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-28794-w) in *Scientific Reports* runs the experiment on a population that better matches the post's scenario: 71 national-level risk analysts (recruited at the closing conference of a National Risk Assessment in a European country) and 118 master's students in risk-related programs. The intervention is short, about 40 minutes, a scripted explanation of decision biases plus the Carter Racing case-study group exercise. The design is pretest/posttest within-subject. The main effect of the intervention on confirmation bias was an *F*(1, 169) of 20.7, *p* < 0.001, partial *η²* of 0.109. Analysts and students benefited equally. That doesn't resolve the long-run-transfer question Swaryandini's authors flagged. It does answer a narrower one: a 40-minute structured-workshop session reduced confirmation bias on the post-test in a group of working national-level risk analysts as well as in students, on the post-test administered in the same workshop.

The four findings together make a coherent picture. Single-session debiasing training, in well-designed studies with feedback and practice, shifts measured bias on the same task by 20 to 30 percent. Field-transfer studies retain the qualitative effect but with smaller magnitudes (Sellier 19 percent). The meta-analytic estimate across the larger educational-intervention literature is a Hedges *g* of 0.26: not enormous, not negligible. And the limitation that runs through all of this is the one Swaryandini's authors name explicitly: real-world decision-making transfer is unresolved. The literature has measured short-term effects and a few field-transfer effects; what it hasn't measured cleanly is whether the trained habits stick six months later in the wild and on different decisions than the ones the training was about.

This shapes section 10. If trainability evidence supported a transformation, the closing move could be a twelve-step program. It doesn't, so the closing move can't be. The post's prescription has to be the smallest, most durable habit that survives the literature's actual effect sizes. Picking a habit that promises more than the literature's effect sizes can deliver would be a soldier-mode move; the prescription needs to come in under what the evidence supports, not above. Section 10 prescribes a single notebook habit, *because* the trainability evidence doesn't support a more ambitious one.

### 10. Darwin's golden rule, operationalized for 2026

Pull the whole post into one move. Write the disconfirming thing down the moment it shows up.

Hedge first, before the prescription. The post is not saying every primary source it cites endorses this exact habit. None of them does, on its own. Darwin documented the practice on himself, in 1876, and reported its effect on his work. The GJP individual-level findings list "frequency of belief updating" as one of seven predictors of forecast accuracy. Galef's five thought experiments are cognitive prompts that ask you to notice contrary evidence in the moment, but they don't prescribe "write it down in a notebook." What the move does is recur across the post's primary sources as the smallest counter-move that's consistent with what each of them is doing. The trainability evidence in section 9 caps how strong a prescription the post can defend; the trainability evidence supports "small but real". A single-habit prescription that comes in under what the literature has measured is the honest closing move. Anything more ambitious would be the post lying about its own evidence.

The protocol fits on a small card.

1. When an observation, fact, or argument arrives that's opposed to a belief you currently hold, write it down. The same day. Plain text file or paper notebook; both work. Tooling doesn't matter; latency does.
2. Date it. Name the belief it threatens. One sentence is enough.
3. Re-read what you've written every few months. The Kunda 1990 retrieval-bias mechanism would otherwise erase it; the timestamped line on the page resists that.

That's it. Section 8's systems-layer moves (precommitment registers, calibration logs, post-publication critique) are larger, more specialized cousins of this single individual move; the personal notebook is the smallest version that survives the brain doing the forgetting. If you later want a calibration log, that's a different artifact built from a different input (timestamped probabilistic forecasts plus their resolutions). If you want a precommitment register, that's another. The notebook is not all of those at once. It's the smallest one, and the one most likely to actually get used.
